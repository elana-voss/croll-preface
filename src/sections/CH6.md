# Chapter VI. Of that one onely chiefe Medicine of the most Ancient Phylosophers

The head names one single chief Medicine of the most ancient philosophers [CH6.0]. This half praises it, says who may seek it, why its holders hide it, attacks those who falsely claim it, and gives the author's own witness and the older philosophers' account of it [CH6.1–50].

## The greatest gift

The author calls this physick the gift “than which a greater gift of wisdom we never read that divine bounty bestowed on man”, and “this most secret Secret of secret Secrets” [CH6.1]; the margin calls it an occult Minerva and a Jewel beyond price [N253]. It purges the “Plannets of inferior Astronomy”, for “it segregateth all extraneous Sulphureity and impure Terrestreity from metallick and Humane bodies”, and it restores human health [CH6.1]. Interpreted: since the bracket names metallic bodies, these planets most narrowly stand for metals.

A further use he withholds: “to say nothing now of the Magicall and super Celestial use” [CH6.1]. He gives only its condition, the influence of the Sun and Moon and a fourth revolution finished upon its native earth [CH6.1; N254].

He rests all this on antiquity and on eye-witnesses of his time. As God is wonderful in all his works, “so doth he usually hide his wonderfull Gifts in wonderfull men”, whose writings are “woven of so many Hieroglyphicall, Magicall, and Mathematicall Coverings” [CH6.1]. Stated: the truth comes under covers. It promises “a removall of old age and renewing of youth, perpetuall health, and honest food and rayment”, but only “to the Pious and holy Phylosophers dignified by Nature and Education” [CH6.2].

## Neither denial nor boast

The author will not deny these wonders, nor revile them, as most do, “as if they were but the speculations of idle men” [CH6.3], a view reported to be rejected. Revilers, “whom the Phylosophers call fools”, are shut out [CH6.3]. Some wait to hear whether he will claim the preparation, as “greedy Phylosophists use to doe” [CH6.4]. His margin answers: “Here I would be taken as an Index, or as one standing at the door to direct others the way they should goe” [N255]. Having promised “I would set down at least those things which I had made tryall of”, he will falsify nothing [CH6.5].

## Who may seek it

The Art is “not of Sophisters, but Phylosophers”, though “none aboundeth with so many Imposters as this” [CH6.6]. Its true seekers are furnished, since “it cannot be that a poor or covetous man should spend his time in the study of Phylosophy”, and, after Solomon's example, pray “not for wealth, but wisdome” [CH6.6]. They love Heaven, secrets and Nature, and refuse no honest labour of the hands [CH6.7–9; N256]. They desire to use the gift for the end the text names itself, “to wit, the Praise and Honour of God, and the good of his needy neighbor”, “in a constant Taciturnity, without pride” [CH6.10].

They are “the Children of the golden doctrine (whose gold is the Omnipotent God)” [CH6.11]. Stated: the text glosses its own word golden. They seek it for men's health, “leaving the mettalls to those who with an impious hunger” long to be rich [CH6.11; N257]. “A Phylosopher must covet Nothing but Wisdome” [CH6.12]. The gift comes “either by the immediate inspiration of the most High, or by the expert ocular demonstration of a wise Master in this Art” [CH6.12].

## Few reach it, and only by God's will

Few reach “the mount of Chymistry” [CH6.13]. None helps another without long proof of his “pure and Harpocraticall Faith” [CH6.14]. The margin asks where such a Phaenix is found, and says entrance is only by revelation or from one “that hath the Secret, & teacheth it while he liveth” [N258]. Voice unclear: Paracel. is cited beside these words.

The seeker “must not be a slave to other mens judgment, nor live upon their purse, and must retire himselfe” [CH6.15], for “Too much acquantance hinder them / Who labour to find out this Gem.” [CH6.16]. The verse's voice is unclear [CH6.16; N259].

The author gives the agreed word of reverend antiquity: none found the secret by wit or experience, for “it is somthing Divine and hid, and that which is above Reason” [CH6.17]; God “hath reavealed and made it plaine to his faithfull wise ones” [CH6.17; N260]. So among thousands “there is not one to whom the doors of Nature are unlockt” [CH6.18], since “it is not expedient for all to their salvation, that they should have Honors & riches with their health” [CH6.18; N261]. This holds “when men seek the Kingdome of God, and study to finde out the Celestiall Stone in the last place” [CH6.18]. Several readings: the Stone is to be sought only after the Kingdom of God; or men put both last, and fail.

Some get “the key of the Phylosophicall Garden (as I know some that have)”, yet cannot enter “because the Gate was bolted, that is, the Divine Grace and Benediction was denyed and did resist” [CH6.19]. Stated: the text glosses its gate. Impostors holding a true ferment “prepar'd by others” could not multiply it, “by reason of the ignorance of the principle or beginning” [CH6.19].

## Why the philosophers write in covers

The work is “hid under the robe of the Phylosophicall Virgin, which even one brother would not teach another” [CH6.20].

The genuine philosophers “doe study and strive to be harsh and rugged” [CH6.21], have “covenanted and sworn they would never write it plainly in any Book”, and none has made it known except to “such who had an understanding allegorically” [CH6.21]. “God would not manifest this knowledg to all, to avoyd offence.” [N262]. They have “power to dispence or give Names at their pleasure” [CH6.21]. Interpreted: their names are not to be read at face value.

What they affirm: none reaches the end “before the fat or blood of the Sun and the Dew of the Lunary, be joyned into one body by the circular wheel of the Elements with the help of Art and Nature in the form of an Hexagon” [CH6.21]. Interpreted: these are covered words [CH6.21; N264]. The margin says the whole business turns as “Gold animated by the Salt of Niture is made the chief subject of the Phylosophers metallick Physicks” [N263]. Several readings beside CH6.11: the seekers leave metals only as a road to wealth, or the note speaks covertly. And all hangs on grace, “for the Spirit proceedeth from Grace, who inspireth whom he will” [CH6.21].

## Against the impostors

Since “all mans endeavor is but vaine except God prosper it” [CH6.22], the author wonders that great men trust “the golden promises of circumforaneous, vitious, and most lying Impostors” [CH6.22; N266]. These Gymnosophists “inrich the eares of credulous men that they may make their pockets poor” [CH6.23], and deal in “painting white and red, and pargetting womens faces” [CH6.24]. Several readings: cosmetics, or a figure for false colour. Alphidius foretold that “instead of the Tincture they should find only the colour, instead of the Hermetick Stone a pibble or glasse” [CH6.25]. The margin adds Lully's curse: “He that publisheth this Art, let him die an evill death, saith Lully” [N268].

## The adept's silence and the last day

The adept, who “hath happily labored like Hermes more then twenty years”, “will be lesse afraid to die an hundred most cruell deaths” than publish the treasure to the unworthy, for he must account for “the Talent committed to him” [CH6.27]. At the last day “the heat of the Centre united to the heat of Sun shall set all corruption of the Elements on fire” [CH6.28], and what is pure remains “like a pure, cleare, incorruptible and fixed Essence in a serene resplendent Chrystalin Earth” [CH6.29]. “The property of fire is to seperate the impurity of the Elements.” [N269]. Interpreted: the last day is drawn in the terms of the medicine's purging.

## A wish for princes

The author wishes princes would set skilled men to the Three Families of Nature, “viz. Animalls, Vegetables, and Mineralls”, “separating them by Fire into their Three First (things.)” [CH6.30]. This would show “the TRIUNE naked DIANA” with “a most limpid or clear MERCURIAL, a most garnished SULPHUREAN or oyly, and a most bright pure SALINE form” [CH6.31]. Stated: the three forms are named as hers. Francis the First meant to try one, “but was prevented by death” [CH6.32]. With “the sole fountaine being reserved to it selfe for its own uses”, the spare streams would serve the poor [CH6.33]. God gives “by asking, seeking, and knocking” [CH6.34; N271].

## The King's highway

“For this onely is the Kings high-way”, to Nature's secrets and to God, “alterity being swallowed up of unity” [CH6.35]. “There is no recovering or returning to what we have lost but by the Crosse and Death” [CH6.35; N272].

## The author's own witness

The author sought it “not to the end that I might make Gold and Silver” and, hindered by envy, says “I comfort my selfe with the cogitation onely” [CH6.36; N273]. Yet he was granted “that I should see and taste it, at some Great mans house” [CH6.37]. Words he does not English follow, with odd capitals, and then: “Which I forbear to English.” [CH6.37]. Several readings: the capitals hide letters to be read apart, or are misprints.

The metamorphoses he saw were made in the cold, “the Moyst way of the Ancients not as yet intensly exalted to the eye of the Basilisk” [CH6.38]. A dying man was saved “by giving one onely drop of that Latex or liquor”, a storehouse “in which the whole world was centred”, in one night [CH6.38]. But “many diseases are a Divine innate punishment, for which there is no cure in Nature” [CH6.38], and “There is no Physitian can cure death, but Christ.” [N275]. The margin's Basilisk “like Lightning suddenly and unawares burneth up any imperfect mettall whatsoever” [N274].

## Metals and bodies alike

Metals are cleansed like men's bodies and “restored to their True Health, which is aureity or goldness”, “for mettals doe not differ in specie but onely in accidents” [CH6.39]. This is “justly not to be made known (though it be truer then true) to our Vulgar sort” [CH6.40]. Their disbelief is a gain, protecting true students [N276].

## Paracelsus and Lullius

The Astral physician needs this to cure “the four Monarchs of all diseases, the Epilepsie, Gout, Dropsie, Leprosie” [CH6.41]. Paracelsus cured them “through the help of Christ, not of the Devill”, “proving in some of his works that God taught him the medicine” [CH6.41; N277]. It heals “as a Carpenter that squareth all kind of timber” [CH6.42]. Lullius, approved, held that “between the Artist and God the first cause, there ought to be an agreement without contrariety” [CH6.44]. Some eminent men before, and “two publick Phylosophers of divers Nations in our time”, came to a wretched end, “answerable to the unworthy publishing of this most True Phylosophicall Art” [CH6.44].

## The ancient philosophers and their silence

The ancients were “these private not publick Secretaries of Nature” who rejoiced “in Quiet Silence” [CH6.46; N279]. Paracelsus is given as calling Phylosophy Adept “Long Life and free from all infirmity, even till the Naturall death” [CH6.47]. Its virtues are “concentred into one masse” [CH6.49]. Those of Hermes school were “altogether dumb in keeping close the secret of the Art by a constant Taciturnity” [CH6.50], speaking only “in a picture of Aenigmaticall words” [N281]. Yet the reason they give is quoted: the Medicine is “the Life and inlightning Light”, “the Fift Essence compounded of the four Elements” [CH6.50].

## The Fift Essence and its names

The author now says what the one chief Medicine is. God created a Fift Essence as “the root of life in Nature for the preservation of the four qualities of the Humane body” [CH6.51]. “The celestiall Fire that burneth not, is the soul and life of all Creatures”, and in it the influences of all the heavenly bodies are gathered into one [CH6.51]. A run of names follows, among them “the Glasse of God's Mysteries, and the Miracle of universall Nature” and “the Son of the Sun and Moon” [CH6.51]. It is “altogether one and the same with the spirit of our body, the bond between the Body and Soul” [CH6.51]. The margin adds “in this Stone the universal Nature of things shineth forth” and “The Tincture is the Fife Essence of the Microcosm” [N283] (note-only).

This matter “hath been altogether unknown to the Physitians of our time”, who “goe not into the Temple of Apollo through the right door, but break through the Roofe” [CH6.51].

## How it cures: equality of the Three First

The author builds on the cause of disease “in the judgement of the best Physitians”: “the enormity of the Naturall proportion of the Three First”, or, “as common Physitians say, the disorder and ill disposition of the four Elements or Humors” [CH6.52]. The Medicine “is in it selfe the matter of our Creation”, and “nor doth it admit any malignant spirit, but they all fly from it” [CH6.52]. The margin gives the reason: “the inequality and dominion of one over another is it that breedeth diseases” [N285]. “Mans health consisteth in the agreement and union of the Three First Substances”; when the Stars set them on fire, “the intestine wars follow” [CH6.53]. And because “the Three First Substances of diseases are valitile”, they “give place to the Essence of Fire which consumeth the disease and separateth the pure from the impure” [CH6.53].

The Fift Essence of the body “reduceth to the true Temperament by making equall the unequall” [CH6.54]. The author states its limit: it keeps a man sound “even to his Naturall death, that is to that Terme of Life which the Omnipotent God hath appointed to Man for disobedience”, and only one “who shall use it prudently and seasonably with a devout calling upon the Name of the Lord” [CH6.54].

## The first in physic

The author calls it “this true Triacle of Phylosophers” [CH6.55]. His ground: “For in every kind there is a certaine One that holdeth the first degree in that kind” [CH6.55]. Made of “the Soule or Spirit of the world, which hath in it the force of all Celestiall and Terrestriall things”, it ranks first in physic, and its user “may live as long as the ancient Fathers” [CH6.55].

## The Spirit of the world and our natural heat

From Sun and Moon, “as Suchtenius learnedly discourseth”, springs the Spirit of the world, so that “there can be nothing in all the world that may or can be without a spark of this Spirit” [CH6.56]. Whose words these are, Suchtenius's or the author's, is UNCLEAR. “This Celestiall Spirit which is one and the same with our Naturall spirit” is our natural heat; when it is hindered, “that breeds bad blood”, and its full dissipation at last is “the Naturall Death” [CH6.56].

So it must be strengthened, and by a rule: “every agent when it begins to act, doth not move toward any thing below it selfe, but to that which is equall like and sutable to it” [CH6.57]. The strengthening is by the celestial heat of Sun and Moon, or by things where their virtue abounds, for from such things “the spirit or that supernall Fire is got out of them by art” [CH6.57]. Two fires are parted: “burning Heat, consuming all things, and darknesse, making all things barren, are proper to the Elementall and Inferior Fire” [CH6.57].

## Why gross medicines fail

“Medicines ought to be preserving and very durable, and remote from corruption” [CH6.58]. “tis but in vaine to think to preserve a corruptible body by a putrid and corruptible thing” [CH6.59]; the margin says “One Like added to its like maketh it more Like.” [N291]. This is aimed at “too many Physitians of our time who labor in vaine to cure Men of their maladies by their grosse and corporall compositions of Medicines”, for “diseases are not corporall, but spirituall and lurking in the spirits” [CH6.59].

One note does not fit: “The vitall spirit in Man & the Elemental is one spirit.” [N292]. Another makes our spirit one with the Celestiall [N290], and the paragraphs set the Elementall against the Celestiall [CH6.57; CH6.60]. The clash rests on the one word Elemental (note-only, wording-dependent).

The vital spirit is “the innate Mummy, and hath its seat in the midst of Mans heart” [CH6.60]. To keep it, “they must seek after not the Elementall, but that Celestiall Heat of the Sun and Moon that dwelleth in the more incorruptible substance that is to be found under the Globe of the Moon” [CH6.60]. It is “prepared and made up into a medicine and most pleasant meat” and taken by mouth [CH6.60]. Such a physician might keep youth to the point of death and, “were it not for the wages of sin, withstand death” [CH6.61].

## Paracelsus cleared

“Paracelsus calleth it the Element of FIRE” [CH6.62]. The author gives his meaning “that of right he may here be vindicated from the unjust calumny wherewith he was branded”: the Balsom of Nature may be preserved “to the last gasp of Life without any sicknesse, paine and griefe” [CH6.62]. He was stopped by “being Poysoned in his body by his malicious and inhuman adversaries” [CH6.62]. The scoff rejected is “that he by this his medicine would presumptously prepare himselfe an entrance and way to the immortall health of his body” [CH6.62]. The author says Paracelsus “came to the Naturall Terme of his Life by an untimely and violent death of a draught of Poyson” [CH6.62], though natural death was earlier “a full extinction, consumption and dissipation of that spirit” [CH6.56]; the strain rests on that wording (wording-dependent).

## The true long life: union with the Centre

The author turns to God. “GOD is the Centre of all Creatures”, and “Blessednesse is in unity, not in the circumference; in Christ, not in the world, is Peace and the Rest of Soules.” [CH6.63]. Whoever is united to that Centre, “he waxeth young rather than old: And this is the true Long Life of the Cabalists, and of Paracelsus”, “the true Enochean Life” [CH6.64]. This reading of long life is Stated by the text itself [CH6.64]. The margin foresees those “who exclaime that this interpretation is forced and far setch'd” [N296]. Earlier, long life meant the body's life by the Medicine [CH6.55; CH6.60–61]; here it is used in a second sense (Interpreted).

Whoever is not united must “be separated from the Light and Life by the second death” [CH6.65]. The true philosophy is to pass into God, “whereby thou mayest be transform'd, and made (as it were) a God” [CH6.66].

The Adept Phylosophers “alwayes look upon these transitory things with the left eye, but on Heaven with the right” [CH6.67]. Their mind “hath ever been far off and estranged from the Cavill above mentioned” [CH6.68]; Interpreted: the cavil is the scoff of CH6.62. They see all in “one simple (intuitive vision or) sight from within, a kind of an Essentiall touch of the Divinity”, a beauty “incomprehensible to the Old Creature” [CH6.68]. Hermes “doth also acknowledge in his Monade” that the knowledge and contemplation of the one and perfect is “a sacred, Heavenly, and hid silence” [CH6.68]. The author says what it is (Stated): “It is the intimate vision of GOD, and the Intuitive knowledge of GOD” [CH6.68].

He names a spiritual death of the Saints, “which the Jewes call the kisse of Death”, “if the fulnesse of Life may be called death” [CH6.68]. “We must die to the World, Flesh, Blood, and the whole Animall Man” [CH6.68].

Nothing is used twice. “the most beautifull Nothing (that is, the shadow and region of Darknesse and Death)” is cast behind [CH6.68]; the margin says “Whatsoever is not God, is Nothing” [N299]. Yet the friends of God cast themselves “into the Sea of Nihilitude or Nothingnesse” [CH6.68]. Several readings: two different nothings, the world left behind and the self given up, which the words on going “out from themselves” support [CH6.68]; or one word used of unlike things. It rests on the word (wording-dependent).

## Two reasons life may be prolonged

“it is repugnant neither to Nature nor Reason that a Man may prolong his life beyond the common ordinary age of Men”, for two reasons [CH6.69].

First, “in NATURALS there is no certaine appointed Terme apparent what day we shall die” [CH6.70]. “I speak here Phylosophically of the Naturall death, which is onely the wasting of the Naturall Moysture and Heat, as may be seen in a burning lamp”, not of the Fatall death God has set [CH6.70]. “As God hath numbred our hairs, so also doth he reckon our years, leaving them in our power”, and a long life is not displeasing to him, yet always short of the term “of DIVINE PREDESTINATION which is unknown to mortalls” [CH6.70]. The margin speaks of “a two fold death, from the Iliad or first principle, and from the Ens or Being” [N307].

Second, God created the Medicine for life. “Paracelsus saith that the death which is from the resolution of the Iliad may be hindred by the industry of the Physitian, but that which is from the Ens or Being cannot” [CH6.71]. Life is fed like fire with wood, but “we being destitute of wisdome know not that wood wherewith our life might be cherished and prolonged” [CH6.71]; wood here stands for the unknown means (Interpreted, from the fire comparison). Adam lived long “by the help of Secrets and by Wisdome which was revealed but to few” [CH6.71], and had the Medicine “in his Family, as Lactantius witnesseth” [CH6.71]. “the Flood did not wast the things that grow, but wash'd away our wisdome of knowing them” [CH6.71], and “These most secret of secrets have ever been hid from the common sort of them that professe Phylosophy” [CH6.71].

## Two ways life is shortened

“the life is broken off, or shortned, two manner of wayes” [CH6.72].

First, by the MIND, through diseases “as Inchantment, Imagination, Estimation, Influence; Superstition” [CH6.73]. Against them avails “onely Faith which is able to resist them”, or magical means, and these diseases “which only Adept Physitians know, are healed without the help of Natural Physick” [CH6.73]. The mind has “a kind of a hidden Virtue, of changing, attracting, and binding that which it desireth”, like the Loadstone that does “(as it were) bewitch spiritually and invisibly” [CH6.73]. Their “malignant Astra's must be averted by a supernaturall cure and magicall help into something else” [CH6.73]. “Godlinesse is the chiefest remedy” [CH6.73].

Second, “by the Being, as by Entall or Corporall diseases” [CH6.74]. “Health is preserved by fastings, and a moderate Diet is the best Governance to prolong the life” [CH6.74]. Each kind needs its own cure: “Corporall medicines doe no good to mentall or supernaturall distempers” [CH6.74].

## The conclusion: what he imparts, and to whom

Not to “wander wider then the bounds of a Preface”, the author closes [CH6.75]. He has imparted “so far as was lawfull, and so much as was allowable by God” to “the Children of Learning and Heirs of Wisdome” [CH6.76]; the margin bids “Read them over and over, & over again, and againe” [N313]. For “it concerns the publick good to make known also in publick writing what belongs to the publick” [CH6.76].

He tells of “above ten years” in the families “of ESNE at Lyons in France, and in that of BAPPENHEIMIE in the Segniory of Mareschall”, of turning over “the BOOK of NATURE (in which every region is a leaf)”, and of his patron, “Heroick Prince CHRISTIAN ANHALTIN” [CH6.76].

He says plainly that he leaves things unhandled and obscure (Stated): “I have not handled all things here in this place, to avoyd prolixity”, and “It is provided for by the Philosophical law that some tedious things should be left obscure to young Schollers and for intelligent & wise men to find out” [CH6.76]. “He that can receive it let him receive it” [CH6.76]; to the sincere reader “the doors and Inner rooms are unlockt” [CH6.76]. “A few things or words are sufficient for him that understandeth.” [N317].

Critics should publish their own, “for surely Physick is not yet come to the Limit of perfection”, and become Bees in a union “divorcing the multitude and abstaining from all fornication” [CH6.76]. Interpreted, narrowest: these words are figurative, following the Bees, for workers kept apart from the crowd. He hopes “that Ancient, True, & Philosophical Medicin” may “be restored to its lost lustre & ancient splendor” [CH6.76]. The Preface ends: “Which that holy Triunity grant, whose unspeakable Name be blessed for ever and ever” [CH6.77].
