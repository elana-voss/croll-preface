# Chapter IV. How, and with what Vertue, Physick worketh upon Mans body and cureth his Diseases

The chapter asks how a medicine works on the body and by what power it cures [CH4.0]. It ends with two stories from Prague, given as instances of what Nature does when she is kept safe [CH4.12–14].

## The question physitians dispute

The author opens with what physitians “commonly dispute and contend for”: “Whether, according to Hippocrates his Maxim, that Contraryes are to be cured with CONTRARYES, or Like with LIKE, according to Paracelsus?” [CH4.1]. Each maxim is given to its owner by name.

## Both maxims can stand

His answer: “these Maxims may be both received in the Anatomy of Nature, though they seem thwart one the other” [CH4.2]. Many miss the philosophers' meaning “because they cannot understand how to reconcile them by a seasonable and sutable interpretation” [CH4.2]. Stated: the text itself says the maxims only seem to be at odds and are to be reconciled by interpretation.

Physick is “nothing else but an opposition of those things that are desired”, namely “a refreshing of the strength and Balsom, and a removing of superfluityes or impurities that cause diseases” [CH4.2]. Paracelsus “therefore doth not find fault with Hippocrates” for saying that “hunger is to to be cured with meat, thirst with drink”, labour with rest, and that contraries cure contraries [CH4.2]; these examples are Hippocrates' words as the author reports them. The quarrel is with Galen: Paracelsus “is poynt blanck against Galen”, who applied the contrariety of Hippocrates “chiefly to those bare and naked qualityes which Hippocrates utterly disclaimed”, and “by an unhappy mistake referred the first and principall Idaea's of Cures to Refrigerations, Calefactions, Humectations, Exsiccations” [CH4.2]. Galen's view is reported in order to be rejected.

## Nature is the physitian

The cure is done by “The onely and alone NATURES of Physicall medicines”, called “those Hippocraticall vertues”; “the Physitian is but the minister or servant” [CH4.3].

The Nature within us is “our Life and Balsom, or balsomicall Mummy, that preserveth the body from all corruption” [CH4.3]. Mummy is thus the book's word for this preserving Life. It works by the Saline moysture, explained as “the inferiour Balsom springing up in the inferiour from the superiour” [CH4.3]. It “is its own proper Physitian in Mans body, who asketh nothing of the outward Physitian but Instauration”, which the text glosses as fortification “applyed to the diseased part by a most pure medicine when the like Nature is not at hand” [CH4.3].

The Medicinall Balsom assists “the Vitall or Radicall and Naturall Balsom” “like a coadjutour or privy counsellour”, “because of the simpathy and common agreement between them” [CH4.3]. Restored, the natural strength works “as an inward and unknown Antidote to chase all its enemies out of its Monarchy by the onely power of the Vitall facultyes” [CH4.3]. The margin note here reads: “Nature cureth with like things.” [N187].

## Against cure by contrary qualities

Two reasons follow. To cure with contrary qualities is “to raise and stir up intrinsick commotions to the utter overthrow of Nature”, already weakened by “intestine quarrells” [CH4.4]. And “Contraryes will not willingly entertertaine each other”; if they do not act on or suffer from each other, “where there is no true action and passion, there can follow no true Naturall effect” [CH4.5].

## Medicine agreeable to the part

Medicines therefore “should not be contrary to the grieved part, but very agreeable to it”, having outwardly the Nature the place has inwardly, “because of the Harmony between the great and little world” [CH4.6]. The text adds: “Man is therefore called a Microcosm or little world, because the whole world preserveth, nourisheth and cureth him” [CH4.6]. When the fruits of Earth, Air, Water and Fire in the Microcosm are sick, “they must be restored by fruits like themselves of the Macrocosm” [CH4.6]. So Nature helps its own Nature: “Nature strengthened and assisted by its own Nature doth more forcibly drive out all its enemyes, seeing every Nature is Naturally the best preserver of it selfe” [CH4.6].

Galen is here called as a witness for the author's side: Nature alone is the physitian of all diseases, “as Galen witnesseth in his 13 Book of Method” [CH4.6]. Nature “is the First Mover of every cure, without whose strength and vigour all Physick is in vaine and to no purpose”, and heals itself “by the innate Mummy” [CH4.6]. Daily experience of wounds is the proof [CH4.6].

## Vertues, not qualities

Now the maxim of contraries is allowed in the author's sense. Those who hold it “are not much mistaken if they have not respect to the qulaityes, but the vertues of a contrary Nature” [CH4.6]. For “there are as many crosse and hurtfull vertues to nature as there be good and healthfull for it”, and the good drive out the hurtful [CH4.6]. Qualities do not: “contrary qualityes, are not rooted out by contrary quallityes, but rather are irritated and provoked to strife by each other” [CH4.6]. The ground is a definition: “Nature is not a quality but a vertue” [CH4.6].

The order of service is stated again: “its not the Physitian that hunteth out the disease, but Nature her selfe (who is the Internall Mummy or inward Balsom)”, helped when she fails by “her servant the Physitian” [CH4.6]. Sometimes “it may be the best medicine not to use any medicine at all but to leave the ooperation to the sole Archaeus or Art of Nature” [CH4.6]. Interpreted (wording-dependent): the word or between them gives Archaeus and Art of Nature as two names of one thing. The margin: “The Inward Physitian doth the work, when the Naturall Physitian faileth.” [N188].

If anyone is preserved in a pestilence by Opium, which is most cold, “it is not by the coldnesse of Opium, but but by the Specifick venemous vertue of the Opium”, which “hath a greater degree of poyson then the occult venomous power of that Pestilence” [CH4.6]. “Thus Nature destroyeth one poyson by another, it subdueth a weak evill by a stronger” [CH4.6]. Of qualities: “As winter doth not destroy summer, nor summer winter, but one gives place to the other, so one quality doth not destroy another”, for “without vertue the quallity is dead and wholly accidentall” [CH4.6].

## The root of diseases

“Roots of diseases in the body of Man are neither hot nor cold”; “those Accidents and Excrements are but the signes of the disease, and not the disease it selfe” [CH4.6]. The most pernicious diseases “do not spring out of the matter of the body, or out of the four Humours, but from the Nature of the Seed or Astra's and Invisible mechanick Spirits of the Three principles”, Spirits that “build their outward house and habitation with shells” [CH4.6]. “These Forgers and Invisible Astra's of diseases were not knowne to the Ancients.” [CH4.6]. Forgers is the book's word for these makers of disease.

## Physick a Spirit, seen by the wise only

“Physick is a Spirit (not a body) which the Magician or wise man only can discerne” [CH4.6]. Stated: the text says this is open to some only. A step of work follows: “the body or Earth of Simples is to be cast away”, and “the vertue Heaven or Astrum of it only to be taken” [CH4.6], so that “the life should worke upon the life, and the Spirit upon the Spirit by sepration of the impure body”, “as the intangible Sun Melteth Snow and causeth it to vanish away” [CH4.6]. “Such is the Nature of all Secrets that they worke without the matter and body because the diseases also are not bodyes” [CH4.6]. The author names this: “This is the true and lively Anatomy.” [CH4.6].

## Cure at the root

“This Mechanick and Forger of diseases is to be subdued and destroyed in his Roote and originall” [CH4.6]. Beside it the margin reads “Paracelsus in the Tincture of medicines.” [N189]: evidence that the words beside it are Paracelsus'. Likenesses follow: “the whole Tree cannot be destroyd in the branch but in the Seed”; grass is hindered from growing “not by evultion but corruption of the Earth” [CH4.6]. A note adds: “Degrees and complexions are not considered in diseases.” [N190]. “Not the smoak arising from fire, but the fire it selfe is to be quenched”; the physitian who cures by complexion is like one who would “extinguish the hurtless flame and let alone the fire in the coals” [CH4.6]. The cure takes in hand the root of the seed, which holds the vertues [CH4.6]. A note names “his Book of ancient Physick” [N191].

## What Paracelsus means by like and contrary

“When Paracelsus saith that like preserve their like, and are destroyed by their contraryes”, “he doth not meane the first nor second qualityes which he alwayes calleth Recollaceous and invalid ones, but the substance”, or at least “the Cherionic, Hippocraticall powers and vertues” [CH4.6]. Stated: the text says what the saying means. The margin note at this point is a gap in the transcription [N192].

Then both maxims get their sense. “Like things are said to be the Remedies of diseases because they are of the same Anatomy of Nature, and because they have the like Signatures, Qualities and Roots” [CH4.6]. “Contraryes cure because they piece up the defects and wants”, “because they appease the spirits and consentaneous impurities with a friendly saturity”, and “because they attempt resolutions, consumptions and tacit ablations” [CH4.6]. Interpreted: this is the reconciling of the two maxims that CH4.2 holds possible [CH4.2]. Interpreted (wording-dependent): one strain remains: Qualities stand among the grounds of like cures, while the same paragraph says that “without vertue the quallity is dead and wholly accidentall” [CH4.6], and CH4.4 rejects cure “with contrary qualities” [CH4.4].

## Salt, Sulphur and Mercury

The margin note to CH4.7 reads: “Diseases are cured by applying that which is proper for them.” [N193]. “That Like are to be preferved with their Like is thus to be understood”: “the Salt, Sulphur and Mercury of the Microcosm is preserved by the Salt, Sulphur, and Mercury of the Macrocosm analogically agreeing together” [CH4.7]. Stated: the text gives its own meaning. Man has “various Sulphurs (one of the head, another of the heart, &c.)”, answered by a like variety in herbs and minerals [CH4.7], and “The ministry of Fire discovereth their agreement, operation, and difference” [CH4.7].

An unnamed “he” “distributeth all materiall diseases according to those three substances of which our bodies are composed” [CH4.7]. Interpreted: he is Paracelsus, the last person named before [CH4.6] and named in the margin beside CH4.9 [N194]. Diseases from kindled Sulphur “he called them Sulphurean diseases, such are all Inflamations and Feavers”; “Those that proceed from moysture he Mercuriall.” [CH4.7].

Mercury “may three manner of wayes be exalted above the Naturall degree”: “By the heat of digestion it is distild, and so causeth all sorts of Apoplexies”; by exercise it is sublimed, “whence follow madnesse and Phrensye”; “by the heat of the stars it is precipitated, and then causeth the Gout in hands and feet” [CH4.8]. Diseases of Salt he calls “Saline and Nitrons” [CH4.8]. “Salt destroyeth health 4 wayes”, by Resolution, Calcination, Reverbertion and Alcalization, breeding ulcers, scabs and itch [CH4.8]. Surfetting destroys the Salt, excess dissolves it, the stars thrust it “out of its degree”, and “such as is the Transmutation such also is the disease” [CH4.8].

## Reading his sayings by their drift

He says “the Sulphur kindled in mans body is to be quenched with the Sulphur of the great world, which harmonically agreeth with the other” [CH4.8]. But “he that considereth to what end he spake it will find that such a remedy will be contrary to the disease” [CH4.8]: his meaning is an appropriate Sulphur from “Natures Garden”, “as Sulphur of Vitriol, Niter, common Salt, and such like” [CH4.8].

Likewise “he saith that Ulcers proceeding from Salts must be cured by Salts” [CH4.9]; yet “if we observe his drift, such Salts are contrary to that which was the cause of the disease”, for “they are incarnative and consolidative, as Frankencense, Mastick, Myrrhe, Aloes” [CH4.9]. Hence “sometime he calls that Salt whatever it be that will melt”, dissolve and be hardened by fire [CH4.9]. Both are Stated: the text says the like-named remedy is in truth contrary, and that Salt is used in a wider sense; the second is wording-dependent.

The margin adds: “All Terresterity is the Salt of Paracelsus.” [N194]. “Cumfry cureth the corrosion of Salt.” [N194]. “Gold thickneth the too much sublimation of Mercury.” [N194]. It closes: “we are to assist Nature against these impediments which are the causes of diseases.” [N194]. These are note-only.

## Three sorts of disease, three rules

“As there are Three from which all Physick proceedeth, Mercury, Sulphur, Salt”, so there are three sorts of disease [CH4.10]: “All Ulcers are cured by incarnating Mercury; All mattery gluish sores by Salt; All Inflamations by Sulphur.” [CH4.10]. And: “This needs no Reasons to confirme it.” [CH4.10].

Here ulcers are cured by Mercury; just before, ulcers from Salts are cured by Salts [CH4.9–10]. Several readings: the two rules disagree; or, Interpreted (wording-dependent), since both remedies are called incarnative or incarnating and Salt is said to name such things, the two rules may name one remedy under two words [CH4.9–10].

## Courteous to Nature

Medicines, “though they be contrary to the disease, yet they must of necessity be altogether courteous to Nature”, “for she seeks a sweet Peace in every controversie” [CH4.11]. Should she sink or miscarry, “the Physitian can doe no good” [CH4.11]; but “if she abide and be kept safe and sound she worketh incredible wonders” [CH4.12].

## Two stories from Prague

The margin vouches: “A stupendious and true Story.” [N195]. At Prague in 1602 a Bohemian countryman “whose name was Mathew, about 36 years of age” would for two years “hide an Iron knife of a good bignesse in his huge and wide throat as in a sheath” [CH4.13]. Once “he swallowed it so far that it went down quite into his stomack” [CH4.13]. After “seven full weeks and two dayes”, “by application of drawing Plaisters made of the Load-stone and other things”, the point began to force its passage “by a naturall impulse”, and at his request it was cut out by “Florianis Mathis of Brandeburge chiefe Surgion of the Kingdome and City” [CH4.13]. The stomach had changed the knife's colour “just as if it had laine so long in the fire” [CH4.13].

That after a few weeks he could eat, drink and sleep without pain is the man's own report to the author, “as he told me” [CH4.13]. By God's help and many men's liberality “he was made as sound as ever he was in his life and cost him nothing (contrary to the determinate assertion of Physitians Aphorismes)”, and “shortly after he was marryed” [CH4.13].

In 1606 at Prague a Silesian, for money, did “swallow six and forty white flints which he gathered at banck side” [CH4.14] and, “without impairing his health, he went up and down getting his living for many years together” [CH4.14].
