Croll's Admonitory Preface — A Reading

Chapter V. The Duty of Natures Minister, the Physitian

The chapter announces one subject: “The Duty of Natures Minister, the Physitian.” [CH5.0].

Free from every sect

The author opens by denying that one ancient holds all learning: “ALL common Philosophy was not bound up in Aaistotle, as P. Ramus hath soundly proved” [CH5.1]. In the same way, “nor was the whole Light of Nature drawne into Galen and limited in him only, witnesse Paracelsus” [CH5.1].

Here the Light of Nature stands beside two other phrases as one thing: no man may deprive another of “the liberty of human ingenuity, that Light of Nature, the power to discern and judge as well as himselfe” [CH5.1]. Then comes a figure: “the Grecian Monarchy is at an end” [CH5.1]. Interpreted (my inference from the naming of Aristotle and Galen just before): the rule of the Grecian authorities over learning is declared over.

The conclusion: “he that would be an Excellent Physitian he must be free from every kind of Sect.” [CH5.1]. He is “not to be tyed. to the opinion of any one Author, but to follow the naked Truth, and subscribe to it alone” [CH5.1]. A couplet given to Horace seals it: “I think and judge as cause I finde” and “My rule is not anothers minde” [CH5.1–2]. The margin sets other lines beside it [N196].

Yet no sect is to be slighted

The author at once qualifies this: “Not that other mens inventions are altogether to be slighted, by stickling onely for one sect” [CH5.3]. All sects may be admitted, in words he gives to Picus Mirandula, whom he calls “the Phaenix of Phylosophers”: “in every one of them there is some thing excellent which is not common to another” [CH5.3].

In his own voice he adds: “There is no book so base and bad but hath some good in it” [CH5.4]. Fabius is quoted: “This latter age, saith Fabius, hath endeavored to make the former more compleat” [CH5.4]. Whose voice carries the rest of that sentence is UNCLEAR.

Secrets still hidden

The margin states: “All Secret are by divine Ordination to be discovered.” [N197]. The text agrees: “Doubtlesse there are more secrets yet concealed in the Treasures of Wisdome and Nature then we perceive” [CH5.5]. They are ordained “for Times and Nations, by an immutable decree” and “are to be sought out by wise-hearted men” [CH5.5]. The reason so much remains: Nature “can hardly be wholly comprehended by any mortall man by reason of the shortnesse of his life” [CH5.6]. Nature is said to be “Circularly”; this rests on one word (wording-dependent) [CH5.6].

Neither the Ancients nor Theophrastus wholly

Therefore “neither the Physick of the Ancients, nor that of Theophrastus, is totally to be rejected” [CH5.7], “nor yet so to be imbraced but that if there be a better found out it also is to be received” [CH5.7]. The rule: “both should be compared together, what is best in both let that be cetained” [CH5.7], “For being but men, they have their failings” [CH5.7]. The margin adds: “Many errours of the Ancients are discovered by dayly experience, which yet hath not attained its end neither.” [N198].

Full knowledge belongs elsewhere: “The holy Spirit alone hath the plenary or full knowledge of all things” [CH5.7]. He gives each man his measure, “reserving many things to himselfe that we might alway acknowledge him to be our only teacher” [CH5.7]. Stated: things are held back, and the text names who holds them and why.

Nature’s minister, and two kinds of philosophers

The chapter’s head returns: “A true Phisitian should be the minister, not a master to Nature” [CH5.7]. That he be a philosopher skilful to cure is given as “the conclusion of Hippocrates & Galen” [CH5.7].

The author then divides the philosophers. Some “after the Vulgar manner will be looking below the Moon after the Elementary Nature of things” [CH5.7]. Others, “more truly deserving the name of Philosophers investigate the Arcana and more secret things of Nature” and “go into the very inner roomes and Sanctuary of Nature” [CH5.7]. Interpreted: the inner rooms are a figure for the secret things named in the same sentence. These men have “the true knowledge and Expeeience of Nature’s Light, which maketh a true Physitian indeed” [CH5.7].

“A Physitian is compleated by 3 things” [CH5.7]: the innate virtue of things that grow of the earth, “the Celestiall influence causing that vertue”, “the uniting of it by Chymistry with the Constellation of the firmament”, and the physician’s dexterity “mediating the same” [CH5.7]. Four items follow the number three; whether the last is a fourth the wording does not settle.

First: the interpreter of Nature

“But first, as Paracelsus saith, let him be the legetimate INTERPRETER of NATURE” [CH5.8]. The margin names a writing: “In Chirurgia magna.” [N199]. In the same sentence, under “as Paracelsus saith”, this interpreter searches out Nature’s “oeconomy, and the universall latitude thereof”, pries into all kinds of creatures, “and then comes to consider and looke into man” [CH5.8].

Whose voice speaks next is UNCLEAR: “Phylosophy teacheth the vertues and qualityes of the Earth and Water, as Astronomy doth of the fire and Ayre” [CH5.9]. The physician needs both, and “Chyromancy, Pyromancy and Geomancy are the Elements of Astronomy and Phylosophy” [CH5.9].

The Theophrasteans are then described by their work. They “contemplate and admire the workmanship of Nature throughout this mighty frame of the whole Creation” [CH5.10], inquire into the heavens, “and lastly into the seeds, principles, dimensions, and instincts of all sublunary bodies” [CH5.10]. Meditation, which the margin defines as “a frequent cogitation” [N201], together with their “earnest desires” [CH5.10], brings them “not onely to understand, but also really to imitate the greatest mysteries and secrets of Nature” [CH5.10].

The two callings are set in order: “When the Phylosopher comes to a stand in the Naturall Light of the Macrocosm, then the Physitian begins” [CH5.11]. His field is “the Analogicall Concordance of the Naturall Light of the Microcosm with that of the great world” [CH5.11].

Secondly: a good Spagirus

“Secondly, a Physitian must be a good SPAGIRUS” [CH5.12]. Stated: the text says what that is, “one that can seperate the pure from the impure, and restore his Patient to health by a wise Alchymicall preparation” [CH5.12]. “As Gold is tryed seven times in the fire, so should a Physitian be p{G160: 1 letter illegible}oved by Chymistry, which sepereteth the good from the bad” [CH5.12]. The good physician is “born of the Light of Nature, to whom Nature communicateth his Experience” [CH5.12].

The margin binds the two: “Phylosophy is the mother of Physitians” [N202].

Paracelsus above all

No man “ever knew and publisht such hard and hidden secrets in all Phylosophy and Physicke” as Paracelsus [CH5.12], “the true Monarch of Physicke, and first Physitian of man” [CH5.12]. He alone “since Noe’s time hath written of the Internall Astrall Man” [CH5.12], and of the origin of diseases “which none of the former Physitians did ever so much as dream of” [CH5.12].

Students of “Heathenish Phylosophy from whence all errour springeth” missed “that twofold unknown body of the Creatures” [CH5.12]. Stated (to wit): a visible body “of the Elements; and the Astrall Sydereal and invisible of the Firmament and the stars” [CH5.12].

A difference stands here: at CH5.7 the physick of Theophrastus is not to be so embraced as to shut out a better, yet here his skill “no man ever yet attaind unto, much lesse exceeded” [CH5.7; CH5.12]. It is recorded, not judged.

Invisible Phylosophy and the New Birth

“The Intellectuall Soul of Man, that divine Light flowing out of that spiracle of God and Divine springs pertaineth to the Invisible Phylosophy, whose foundation is CHRIST” [CH5.13]. So philosophy must be “Christian-like, not after the manner of the Heathen in hollow empty language and temporaneous Arts” [CH5.13]. Knowing Nature is not the whole task: by “the supernall help of the Light of Grace” we are to possess eternal life, and “this is true Theologicall Phylosophy” [CH5.13]. Hence “the New Birth is first to be sought for, and then all other Naturall things will be added without much labour” [CH5.13].

Back to Theophrastus: an ancient art

“But let us return againe to Theophrastus.” [CH5.14]. He was skilled in Chymistry, “though he were not the Auther of that Art” [CH5.14]; earlier philosophers were famous in it, “from whom Paracelsus hath secretly borrowed many things” [CH5.14]. “For that noble Pyronomicall Art hath been most ancient”, once “known only to Kings Princes, & some few of most diligent searchers out of Naturall Phylosophy” [CH5.14].

Paracelsus, “that Monarch of Mysteryes” [CH5.14], restored a lost doctrine, Stated as “the True and Philosophicall Physick” [CH5.14]. He laboured to unfold its “great obscurity” and to recall the sciences “from the compasse of the whole circle unto the one only Centre” [CH5.14]. Against this the Devil stirred up “his Emissaries and catch-poles” [CH5.14].

Cures laid to the Devil

The margin to CH5.14 says: “The Physitian that God maketh can doe all things.” [N206]. Theophrastus “by peculiar influence was born a Physitian” [CH5.15]. His enemies, “like the Pharisees of old”, ascribe his gifts to the Devil, “as though the wicked one were the worker of those cures” [CH5.15]: their view, reported to be rejected. The author replies that they “make the Devill stonger then the blessed and glorious God” [CH5.15]; the Devil “hath no power over the Will and Understanding of Man” [CH5.16].

It does not become the German physicians to “contemn those Secrets which God hath granted to their Nature” [CH5.17]; as the Aristotelians rose against Ramus, “even so doe the mingle mangle Physitians against Theophrastus Paracelsus” [CH5.17].

His Divinity and the two Lights

They also attack “his Divinity, which they have neither seen nor read” [CH5.18], though “he onely is able to judge of Truth who is inspired with Divine Wisdome” [CH5.18]. What his divine writings sought to make known is Stated (that is): “that our mind should be raised up to God” [CH5.18].

Then the two Lights: “The Light of Grace, begetteth a true Theologer, yet not without Phylosophy” [CH5.19]; “The Light of Nature, which is the Treasury of God confirmed in the Scriptures, maketh a true Phylosopher” [CH5.19]. Hence “every true Theologer is a Phylosopher, and every true Phylosopher is a Theologer” [CH5.19].

The third school and the treasure within

Others came after Paracelsus, “such as Paulus Brawn of Norimberge, Valentinus Weigelius, and Petrus Winzius” [CH5.20], taught in “the third school of perfect Men, that Mentall or Intellectuall school of Pentecost” [CH5.20]. Their manuscripts “will in their time come to Light, unlesse the indignity and ingratitude of the world keep them back” [CH5.20].

Readers are to turn “into the Temple of the heart” and fetch out “that huge Talent and Treasury which is hid and shut up within themselvs” [CH5.20], not “seek those things without them by many books, mortall masters, long journeys” [CH5.20]. That God enlighten the mind is “the very determinate and appointed End of the Cabala or secret wisdome” [CH5.20]; the text glosses Cabala itself. Books here differ from CH5.4, where every book has some good; the things sought may differ, so this is recorded only [CH5.4; CH5.20].

“The heart of a Regenerate man is Gods Eden” [CH5.21]. God is not now discerned “by reason of the Dark Point of the Quaternary” [CH5.21]; the world is renewed “by the Ternary of Man” [CH5.21]. Interpreted (narrowest reading): the Ternary is the threefold Regeneration “according to Soul, Spirit, and Body” named next [CH5.21]. The Soul turned into the Mind “hath no need of outward teaching” [CH5.23], and “The Academicall spirit cannot not understand this Mystery” [CH5.23].

Withholding, and the defence of obscurity

The author stops himself: “nor may I in this unfit place openly speak more of these Mysteries” [CH5.24]. Stated: he withholds, and says so.

Returning to Paracelsus, he answers those who hate him for “using such words which every one doth not understand” [CH5.25]. He pleads that “the unusuall custome of Phylosophers plead for the strange novelties of names” [CH5.25]. Stated reason: philosophers have always driven off “those sluggish and lazy Drones” [CH5.25]. Plato is quoted: “Arts wrapt in Riddles safe doe lye” [CH5.26].

Finally, let any man ask “if he had that which Theophrastus knew, whether he would tell it to every man” [CH5.27]; to do so would “break the Oath of Hippocrates” [CH5.27]. This differs from CH5.12, where Paracelsus “knew and publisht” hidden secrets [CH5.12]; recorded, not judged.

A secret kept, and handed only to sworn disciples

The author says plainly: “A Secret indeed is to be concealed, it belongs onely to God to reveale it”, for disclosure “commonly it exposeth to death, or prison, or reproach” [CH5.28]. “The true Hermetick Phylosophers took an Oath” not to “defile the virginity of Nature”, yet “have they bequeathed their secrets to their sworn disciples”, and even these “are with much sweat to be searched out” [CH5.28].

Stated: the secrets pass to sworn disciples [CH5.28]. Interpreted: “the virginity of Nature” is a figure; the narrowest reading is a state of Nature “which hath been preserved from the begining of the world”, which the oath binds them not to defile [CH5.28].

Whom Paracelsus condemns, and what true Method is

Men of shallow judgement think Paracelsus would be “the sole and onely Physitian in all the world”; the author reports this to reject it [CH5.29]. In his own voice: “he only condemns the common rable of Physitians who are altogether ignorant both of Theory & Practice” [CH5.29].

The “fallacious Medodists” charge “the genuine Chymists that they have lost the right method”; the author calls it false, holding that “Paracelsus observed onely that method that was agreeable to Reason and Experience” and that “we are not so much to regard by whom, as what it is that is spoken” [CH5.30]. He defines: “True Method consisteth in the knowledg of the Disease, and the Cure of it” [CH5.31].

Three respects; the first opens on Tartar

Wimpeneus “learnedly sheweth that greatest and most grievous Diseases now a dayes are in three respects cured by the Paracelsians” [CH5.31]. The three follow as First, Secondly, Thirdly; their voice is UNCLEAR, Wimpeneus’s or the author’s, though within the first the author speaks as “I will speak a little more of it” [CH5.32; CH5.45; CH5.46].

First, “the Diseases are more perfectly known”: while all were reduced to the four humours, diseases from Tartar could not be cured [CH5.32]. The author turns aside, “since we are here fallen upon Tartar”, and closes the turn with “So much for Tartar.” [CH5.32; CH5.44].

How food is separated

“The first Ens or Being toward life” arises “by the Archaeus, or digestion of the Stomack” and is reduced to “Sulphur, Mercury, and Salt, as is plain in the three chief Emunctories”: urine, entrails, sweat [CH5.33]. A margin note beside it says “The first digestion of the stomack is not digestion, but apparation for the digestions of the particular members” [N217].

The Archaeus is Stated: the stomach is “the instrument of Mans Archaeus, or the Internal, innate Chymist which God hath planted in Man” [CH5.34]. All food holds a Tartar “hurtful to our Health”; a weak stomach sends the impure to the liver, which “seperateth the pure from the impure, viz. the Rubie from the Chrystall” [CH5.34]. The two names are Stated: “The Rubie is the nourishment of all the members”; the Christall becomes urine [CH5.34]. If the liver is weak, the Spirit of the Salt coagulates the matter in the kidneys “into Sand or Tartar” [CH5.34].

The definition: “Tartar therefore is the excrement of meat and drink, which by the spirit of Salt is coagulated in Man” [CH5.35].

Kinds of Tartar, and how they are known

“Paracelsus distinguisheth Tartar into that which is strange or forraine proceeding from meat and drink, and that which is innate of the cruor or hereditary blood” [CH5.36]. The innate kind yields “onely by the Quint-Essence of Gold which reneweth the whole body”; voice UNCLEAR, Paracelsus’s or the author’s [CH5.36]. Tartar “is the mother of almost all diseases of all coagulate bodies” [CH5.37].

Four sorts spring “from the fourfold fruits of the Elements”: Earth, Water, the flesh of beasts and fowls, and the Firmament [CH5.38–42]. From the last come “Astrall diseases”, which “the universall medicine powerfull expelleth” [CH5.42]. This four is counted by source; the “four kinds of Tartar” of CH5.35 by form in the body, “the stone of the bladder, the Sand of the Kidneys” and the rest [CH5.35; CH5.38].

The kinds “shew themselves in the Urine, and are judged of by the art of separation”, and “The disease is cured by the same Element that was the cause of it.” [CH5.43].

The heat that separates

What parts Tartar from nourishment is “our Naturall Heat, or the microcosmicall heat of the Sun and Moon that is in us” [CH5.44]. Weak, it must be strengthened “by the macrocosmicall Heat of Sun and Moon, if it could be got”, that is, “the most simple matter which the most Great God generated of the spirit of the world” [CH5.44]. Stated: that spirit is “one and the same with the spirit of our body” [CH5.44; N220]. Interpreted: “if it could be got” marks that matter as hard to obtain; the alternative the text gives is “those things wherein the power of the Sun and Moon is” [CH5.44].

Hence “the Archaeus of the stomack must destroy the Tartar of the Elements least it be turned into the Tartar of Man”, for “the spirit of the Sun which is Lord and Master of coagulation in various subjects will breed the Stone” [CH5.44]. In CH5.35 it is the spirit of Salt that coagulates Tartar [CH5.35; CH5.44].

The second and third respects

“Secondly, Because we have more perfect medicines now then heretofore, that is to say, of Mineralls”, whose preparations were “discovered to those skilfull Chymists the Sons of Cadmus”; Paracelsus “would have a steely medicine applied as proper for an iron disease” [CH5.45]. Thirdly, “the Harmony between the great and little world” shows which medicine suits which member: Gold, Pearles, Saffron for the heart [CH5.46]. Interpreted: the little world is man, since the author of diseases “afflicteth us, by destroying the frame of the little world” [CH5.44].

Physicians quarrel among themselves

“Whereas Paracelsus offendeth Galen, Galen did the same to others” [CH5.47]. Five disputes are named, such as Argenterius against Fernelius [CH5.48]. The author asks how many millions “will still perish” before they agree [CH5.49].

Read Paracelsus, but not as an oracle

He exhorts “all Students of Hippocraticall and Galenicall Physick” to read “this new Phylosopher and Physitian” with diligence, like bees “segregating the best from the worst” [CH5.50]; not as “Gospel-Oracles”, since “he himselfe hath recanted something of his over hasty writings” [CH5.51]. To those who “throw away the kernell and pick up onely the husks”, he says: pray “for the spirit, that ye may understand the dead letter of Theophrastus” [CH5.53]. Interpreted: the husks answer to “the dead letter”, the kernel to what “the spirit” lets one understand [CH5.53].

Why Paracelsus wrote in a magical style

Against the charge of contradiction: “he wrote in a Magicall stile; not for the vulgar sort, but for himselfe and the understanding children of Wisdome” [CH5.54]. Had he written plainly, vulgar Alchymists would have “prostituted the Art” [CH5.54].

Stated: “He concealed his mysteries under vulgar and various names”, so “we must not take the similitudes for the truth it selfe” [CH5.55]. Reading them needs “a Delian swimmer, a most acute and sharp wit, a Magicall Understanding” [CH5.55]. Magick, a word with one letter unread in the print, is Stated: not witchcraft, but “the plenary knowledge of occult Nature”, working “by a fit application of Agents and Patients, observing the consent and dissent of things” [CH5.55].

Physician and surgeon in one, working by hand

“every Surgeon should be a Physician, and every Physitian a Chyrurgion, that there may be a sound Bridgroom for a sound Bride” [CH5.56]. The physician prepares his own medicines, “to reduce them to their pure simplicity”, for “neither is Sugar free from faeculent impurity” [CH5.56]. “judgement without practice is altogether barren and fruitlesse”; Nature hands remedies “to her Servant to make them up” [CH5.57]. The Rationall Physitians, who cry out against the Chymists “as if they were no Physitians”, stand helpless at the bed “because they can prepare their medicines but onely with their Reason, not at all with their hand” [CH5.57].

False followers

The author will not defend those who claim Paracelsus but “neither acknowledge the minde of the Author in his Theory, nor doe any thing praise-worthy in Practice” [CH5.58]. The “false and lying Theophrasteans” cure “the party and his disease at once with a turse of the Church-yard” [CH5.59]. Interpreted (wording-dependent): the cure is the patient’s death. Though they “doe sometime exceed many Galenists”, “no conscientious man will ever approve” their practice [CH5.60].

Old and New Physick reconciled

Since “the Paracelsian Theory of Chymicall Physick as yet is wrapt up in perplexities and some obscurities”, students should learn of the Chymists to prepare medicines but “observe the method of Hippocrates in curing diseases”, so “these two Schools of Old and New Physick may be reconcil’d without contradiction” [CH5.61].

Prayer, and Physick made common

What an honest physician gives “is to be accounted of as the hand of God” [CH5.62]. Many physicians cannot endure that Physick be imparted to all, lest, made common, “or as they say Prophaned, they should lose their gaines” [CH5.62]. The author calls it a foolish conceit of the vulgar “that a Secret looseth its vertue when tis made known”, and says the gain-getters will cease murmuring when they consider “that neither God nor Nature hath made every man a Physitian” [CH5.62]. The margin: “Paracelsus would not have Physick be concealed.” [N227].

Interpreted: CH5.62 does not deny the dangers that CH5.28 gives for disclosing a secret; what it rejects is the belief that telling destroys a secret’s virtue [CH5.28; CH5.62].

Physicians should imitate the Hermetick Physitians, “who always use to pray for a blessing upon their endeavours”; to use a creature without a blessing God counts “theft and rapine” [CH5.62]. Pray before, give thanks after; their neglect is “the chief cause of thier ill successe” [CH5.63–65].

Eight causes why the best medicine fails

First, the term of life. “No Medicine can preserve the corruptible body from death”, yet “there is one thing that hindereth corruption, reneweth youth, and prolongeth the short life, as in the Patriarchs”; even “the universall and chiefest medicine is of no effect” against the appointed end [CH5.68]. Several readings of the unnamed “one thing”: the universal medicine of the same paragraph [CH5.68]; or the Quint-Essence of Gold, “which reneweth the whole body”, by the same verb [CH5.36].

Second, unskilful physicians have made the disease incurable; those who let a patient perish rather than yield credit men “commonly (and justly) call Honest Hangmen” [CH5.69]. Third, “the Physitian is sent for too late” [CH5.70]. Fourth, the sick breaks “the Golden law of the Locrians” [CH5.71]. Fifth, hard natures and adverse season: “A ripe pear or apple will fall of its own accord” [CH5.72]. Sixth, “what Nature hath once taken away the Physitian can by no means restore” [CH5.73]. Seventh, the patient’s meanness and doubt; “There is a twofold Physick; Visible or created, and Invisible even the WORD of GOD” [CH5.74].

Faith and imagination

Within the seventh cause: Stated, “Naturall Faith (I speak not of the Faith of Grace by Christ) inborn of God the Father in the first Creation, which is our IMAGINATION” can cause and cure diseases [CH5.75]. In plague, fear “breedeth that Basilisk of Heaven”, and “the Iliaster or Evestrum of the Sun” punishes sin [CH5.75].

“Faith giveth the Imagination”, and “the Imagination giveth a Sydus or Constellation” [CH5.76]. The Spirit that helps is Stated: “I mean the Spirit of Nature or the Sydus of the medicine” [CH5.77]. A new spring in Misnia and Bohemia cures by “the constant excessive affection of them that use it” [CH5.77]. Avicen is quoted: “Nature obeyeth the cogitations and vehement desires of the Soul” [CH5.78]. “God will doe nothing without Man, as his Organ or Instument” [CH5.80].

Eighth, lest the healed sin more: “every sicknesse is as it were an expiation or propitiatory of sacrifice” [CH5.81].

Diseases from the wrath of God

Here no bodily medicine helps, “because God hath shut the doors of Nature”; the cure is repentance through “the Celestiall Physitian of our Soules” [CH5.82]. Paracelsus calls them “Deal diseases, which are from the Ens of God”: “the Cure, saith he, is by Faith” [CH5.82]. The margin: “A Physitian beginneth in appeasing Gods wrath.” [N252].
