Croll's Admonitory Preface — A Reading

Now by God’s assistance I shall say something concerning the Generation, Dignity, and Excellency of the Microcosm, or Little World, Man

The heading names one subject three ways, Microcosm, Little World and Man [MICRO.0]. The section runs from knowing oneself, through the making and parts of man, to the invisible man within him, his Imagination and a kind of Magick, and ends by naming those who cleared this foundation.

Knowing oneself

The author opens with a definition: “the most excellent Phylosophy is that which enlightens the mind to the right knowledge of it selfe” [MICRO.1]. He gives Trismegistus the saying that ignorance “is the greatest Enemy and principall Tormenter in every Man” [MICRO.1]. A warning follows to the man “who considerest not the Treasure that is hid in thy earthen vessell, and may thereout be digged” [MICRO.1]. The lesson God has set is printed in capitals, “KNOW THY SELFE”, which Agryppa ties to the Oracle of Apollo at Delphos [MICRO.1].

Self-knowledge comes first because man holds all things and the Creator’s image in himself; knowing himself, he may “as in a kind of Deified glasse, behold and understand all things” [MICRO.2]. This lasting knowledge is not got from books or reasoning: the mind is perfected “by a passive reception of Divine things; not by study and paines; but by patience and submission” [MICRO.3]. “The Notion of all things is created together with us and in us”; sin has put us to sleep, and “we are onely to awake out of our slumbring and snorting” [MICRO.3]. The author’s own proof: Christ would not have commanded us to seek unless the treasures of wisdom were laid up in us before [MICRO.3].

Interpreted (this page’s inference): the treasure hid in the earthen vessel is what MICRO.3 calls the notion of all things hidden in us; MICRO.16 speaks again of the “Talent and Treasure laid up in his Earthen Vessell” [MICRO.1; MICRO.3; MICRO.16].

Self-knowledge and the knowledge of God

We are to know ourselves “according to both kinds of Light.” and the next words are numbered, “(1) according to the Spirit and Nature” [MICRO.4]. Two readings are defensible: (a) the two Lights are Spirit and Nature, under one number; (b) the numeral opens a list, and Spirit and Nature stand under its first number only. MICRO.17 again says the knowledge of man is to be taken “from both Lights” [MICRO.17].

The knowledge of God rises at once from self-knowledge, “for neither can be absolute & compleat without the other” [MICRO.4]. Dionysius is given the view that “we cannot know God from his own Nature, but from that most orderly disposing of all the Creatures proceeding from himselfe” [MICRO.5]. Ignorance of God is “the Rise or Spring of all diseases, the root of wickedness” [MICRO.6]; to know him is life, as our Saviour says, “This is life eternall to know the Father the true God” [MICRO.7], and one who knows God in Christ becomes his temple “and so is Deified” [MICRO.7].

Against the philosophy of the Heathen

Hence “what the Heathen wrote concerning Nature is for the most part false” [MICRO.8], though many were “seduced by ignorance rather then malice” [MICRO.8]. The rule: “All true Phylosophy should be grounded on the Scriptures and so return into God” [MICRO.8]; “No Art can be perfected without Regeneration” [MICRO.8]. The margin: “Christians should not be ruled by Heathen Phylosophy.” [N089]

An order of steps follows: “For Faith, Hope and Love, follow knowledge, Adhaesion or cleaving to followeth Love, Union follows Adhaesion, in Union is Blessednesse and Wisdome.” [MICRO.9] Hermes and others of godly life before the Word was incarnate knew this Regeneration, “though they concealed it among other Secrets, they knew it better then many of us who call our selves Christians” [MICRO.9].

Man between God, Angels and the world, and his fall

Man stands “between Time and Eternity” [MICRO.10]: God above, the Angels beside him, “from whom he differeth onely in body and the Judgement to come”, and below “the visible World whereof he is a pattern” [MICRO.10]. He is “a true Proteus of a fickle & wavering disposition”, able by grace to be “regenerated into a quiet Angell” or, creeping downward, to “degenerate into a restlesse Bruite” [MICRO.10].

Turning from the mean to himself, man “learned by experience what his own proper Evill and Nothing was” [MICRO.10]; “like Lucifer, not content with his lot” he set himself against God and was “cast out of the Garden of pleasures into this dolefull and darksome valley of Misery and Ignorance” [MICRO.10].

How man was made: the bond of the world

“Man, the bond or buckle of the world” was made last, on the sixth day, from the slime of the Earth, which the text explains as the purest substance of the whole frame of the world drawn into one body [MICRO.11]. He was “made to supply the place of the fallen Angells” [MICRO.11].

“As an Oration is made up of letters and sillables, so the Microcosm or Limus Terrae, Man is compacted of all bodies and created things.” [MICRO.12] God “took the Quintessence out of all things created” and made man of it [MICRO.12], and the creatures honour him as “Gods steward set over the Orchard or Garden of this world” [MICRO.12].

The figure of Centre and Circle: God “is the Centre in that all things flow from him”, and “He is the Circle, because like an all-capacious Tabernacle he concludeth and comprehendeth all things.” [MICRO.12] So too: “Thus Man in imitation of his Creator is the Centre of the Creatures, and the Circle of them all.” [MICRO.13] Centre, because all creatures pour their virtues on him “as upon a middle Point and Retinacle or that by which they are stayed and supported”; circle, because he “leadeth back all things that gushed out of that Summum Bonum, or chiefest good, unto the fountaine of Eternity” [MICRO.13].

The chain of images and the parts of man

A descending chain: “The world was the first figure or image of God, Man is the image of the World”, and so down to stones [MICRO.14]. The author approves an old name: “the prudent Ancients wilsely called Man a Microcosm or little World, which few now a dayes understand, that the great visible World was made Man” [MICRO.14]. Stated: the text gives the sense of the name itself.

Man is twofold, visible and invisible; he has two Spirits, “one a syderiall Spirit from the Firmament, the other from the breath of life” [MICRO.14]; and “Man hath three parts, a mortall Body with a Syderiall Spirit, and an immortall Soule” [MICRO.14]. Three ways of living follow. By flesh and blood only, “he is but a Brute or Beast”, a matter deferred to “my Treatise of Signatures” [MICRO.15]. “If he live Rationally, then he is a Man” [MICRO.15]. By the God-like Spirit, “then hath he dominion over the Stars and all things else” [MICRO.16], for “He was made of the world, he beareth the world about in him, and is borne of the world.” [MICRO.16]

Seed, two Fathers, and the third mean

The first matter, “which Phylosophers call the Chaos and Hylen, or Mother of the world”, was the seed of the great world, as the great world is the seed of man [MICRO.17]. As “in every graine or seed of the Tree there lies hid another Tree”, so the first matter, “which Paracelsus calleth the Limbus, whose Earth was the WORD of the Lord”, was the seed of all things to be created, and man came last as the perfect seed [MICRO.17].

“Man hath two Fathers, an Eternall whose Image he beareth, and a Mortall one, which is the whole world with all the creatures” [MICRO.17], the Limus Terrae, called a “hidden Secret thing” [MICRO.17]. Between the body from the Elements and the soul stands a body from the Firmament, the soul’s vehicle, a “third mean which partaketh of the other two” [MICRO.17]. Through it the soul “is first poured and descendeth into the middle poynt of the Heart, which is the Centre of the little world” [MICRO.18]. “The whole Firmament is in us with the Planets and Stars” [MICRO.18], “so that of the syderiall spirit of the Firmament we may learn all Naturall things” [MICRO.18].

One in person, three in essence

“As God is One in Essence, Trine (or Three) in persons, so Man is One in Person, Trine (or Three) in distinct Essence” [MICRO.20]: earthly body, heavenly spirit, and the soul God breathed in. The witnesses are Scripture and “the unanimous consent of all that truly professed Phylosophy from the Light of Nature” [MICRO.21]; the margin adds Paul, who “shewes three parts of Man, Spirit, Soule and Body” [N114].

Even one who denies the three parts must grant the body from the earth and the breath of life, “which is that Linum Caelorum, or slime of the Heavens from the Lord” [MICRO.22]. The life in the body “is the Syderiall Spirit, and is properly the Man, not flesh and blood” [MICRO.23]; in the soul dwells “the Spirit of the Lord, the WORD of God, the eternall man” [MICRO.24]. So “it followeth that there are three worlds or Heavens in Man”, and “some have called him the Fourth World” [MICRO.24]. The author asks: “for what else is the Spirit of Man which God breathed into him but God himselfe dwelling in us?” [MICRO.24] And, on a condition: “if he be a true Magician, he is not inferiour to the Angells in all Magicall operation” [MICRO.24].

Five things man partakes of

Five numbered heads follow [MICRO.25–29]. (1) With God: “the Intellectuall Soul is a certaine particle of the Divine Soule” [MICRO.25]. (2) With the Angels, for he is “Gabalis socius Angelorum, a curdled companion of the Angells, or one mixed with in fellowship” [MICRO.26]. (3) With the Firmament, from which comes the astral spirit; but “This Spirit is mortall; onely the Intellectuall Soule which God breathed into man is immortall.” [MICRO.27] (4) With the Elements, of which his body is made; the world, “the Parant of Man according to Paracelsus”, holds four Elementary Spirits and a fifth kind [MICRO.28]. (5) With all animals, vegetables and minerals [MICRO.29].

Hence man is the most excellent creature, and he rises so far that “he is made the Son of God, transformed into the same image which is God and made one with him” [MICRO.30], a thing “not granted either to Angells, or the world, or any other Creature, but to Man only” [MICRO.30].

The Sydereall or invisible man

The author now treats the invisible man more fully: “If Aristotle had taken notice of this Olympick Spirit, and Galen made more account of it, there had not such errors crept into Philosophy and Physick” [MICRO.31]. “Thus Adam had two bodies, that is, a visible Elementall, and an invisible Sydereall body.” [MICRO.31] At every birth the visible man is the instrument of “an incorporeall Astralick man which moveth, guideth, and performeth all skilfull matters” [MICRO.31], and it is “the invisible Sydereall body that is capable of attaining Phylosophy” [MICRO.31].

This inner man is one with the Firmament, “like rednesse with wine, whitenesse with snow” [MICRO.32]. It has many names, each with its reason: the Genius; “it is called Penates, because it is in our power and born with us”; Paracelsus his Adech, “that which first inwardly formeth in our mind what we afterward outwardly fashion with our hands”; and the imagination [MICRO.32].

The author explains his word Astra’s: not “the seaven visible coales of Heaven which are but the bodyes of the Astra’s”, but the invisible body of all things; and “they are nothing else but the verue or powers of the Angells” [MICRO.32]. Stated: the text gives this sense itself. His example: “the seeds of any graine or Wheat is the Astrum, viz. the invisible body” [MICRO.32]. “Bodyes are nothing else but the Excrement of the Astra’s” [MICRO.32], and “There is no body without an Astrum” [MICRO.32].

Imagination

Man’s imagination is all the Astra’s, “the Gate of Wonders, the spring and originall of all Magicall operations” [MICRO.33]. The child in the womb is marked by the imagination without touch, and “Thus the Imagination breedeth the Plague and such like Firmamentall diseases; it brings sicknesse and health.” [MICRO.33] The sydereal spirit “is the Load stone and hath a magnetick nature in man” [MICRO.34].

“This fourth kind of Naturall Magick called Gamahaea doth all things invisibly and spiritually, by the help of Art, which Nature is able to doe visibly and corporally without such help.” [MICRO.35] “There is nothing so hidden in the world which is not praefigured in the Astra’s” [MICRO.35]. As a cloud rises in a clear sky, so in the first Creation all things came “out of the DIVINE NOTHING, or invisible Cabalisticall Poynt, into something” [MICRO.35]. The Firmament too has an Imagination, “but without Reason” [MICRO.35].

Man, the Quintessence of the greater world, “may not onely imitate Heaven, but rule it also at his beck” [MICRO.36], on a condition: “when by holinesse we draw vertue from him who is the true Archetype” [MICRO.36]. Such works are done by Imagination “through the Naturall inborn Faith”, and “The Imagination of Man is the Loadstone that attracteth above a 1000 miles off” [MICRO.36]. The margin limits it: “By Faith we may do good or evillus God permitteth.” [N126]

Gabalisticall Magick and its three Pillars

“Thus by Imagination and true Gabalia all hearbs, all growing things, all mettalls may be produced. This part of Magick is called Gabalisticall, and is supported with three Pillars.” [MICRO.37] First, inward prayer, “not with noyse of words, but in a sacred silence, without opening the mouth and groaning” [MICRO.38]. Second, “NATURALL FAITH, or in-born Wisdome”, given to all men alike [MICRO.39]. Third, “a strongly exalted IMAGINATION”, shown in Jacobs Rods and in pregnant women, which draws other men’s fancy, “as we see in those that gape and yawn” [MICRO.40].

Two outcomes stand side by side. Some by melancholy imagination “have exposed themselves to the temptation of the uncleane Spirit” [MICRO.42]; others, by firm faith and prayer, “on a sudden they have become the Temples of the living God” [MICRO.43]. The margin: “True Faith is the cure of a false Imagination.” [N130]

The sum, and where the author breaks off

“The Sum of all is, that we worship God devoutly in honesty and holinesse” [MICRO.44], as “the more secret Theosophers or wise-hearted to God well know” [MICRO.44]. Inward prayer “uniteth the mind with God, and learneth and knoweth all things of God” [MICRO.44]. Then the author breaks off: these contemplations “will seem harsh and crabbed to the rude and vulgar sort of men; for few read them, and fewer understand them”, and “they require a larger narration then can be made of them at this time” [MICRO.44]. Stated: the text says it gives only part, and why.

Who cleared this foundation

All Chymists, he says, should know “this true Fundamentall of the occult Phylosophycal Physick”, because of “the Harmonicall concord and conspiration between the superiour and inferiour things of the greater and lesser world” [MICRO.45]. Next to Paracelsus he ranks Petrus Severinus the Dane, with Pratensis, for his “Idaea of Paracelsean Physick”, and names Joseph Quercetan, Th: Bovius and Th: Muffet as having confuted Severinus’ adversaries, “the best Hermetick Physitians of this age” [MICRO.45].
