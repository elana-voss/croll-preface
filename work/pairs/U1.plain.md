# The True Physic in Plain Language

This page describes what the author's preface says about the True Physic, the true medicine that cures people's diseases and that few physicians know. It draws on this book alone. It gives no quotes and no passage numbers, and it avoids the book's special words. The page titled The True Physic in Plain Terms gives the evidence for each point. [OPEN.24]

## Is this a process?

An earlier reading of the whole preface finds that the book describes a process here. A whole chapter is headed by how the True Physic, which is wrapped in bark and rind, is got out and prepared by fire, and the preface announces the same in its list of headings. The book names an art that teaches how to tell apart and separate the good from the bad. It also names three steps in a row as one way: making fine, drawing out and separating. Their aim is that a pure, clear matter can be given to our bodies. This finding belongs to that earlier reading; the book does not say of itself that it describes a process. [CH3.0; OPEN.26; CH3.14; CH3.40; CH3.7]

## In brief

1. The author gives the True Physic its own headings: what it is, where it lies hidden, how it is got out and prepared by fire, how it works, and what kind of physician it needs. [OPEN.24; OPEN.25; OPEN.28; CH1.0; CH2.0; CH3.0; CH4.0]
2. Every natural body breaks down into three parts, no more and no fewer, and the art of separating shows this. [CH1.19]
3. The True Physic lies in plants, animals and minerals. It is not an outward quality but a particular hidden power, which the Creator planted in the seeds. [CH2.2]
4. It is wrapped in bark and rind and must be got out and prepared by fire. Without chemistry all medicine is lifeless. [CH3.0; CH3.8]
5. Nature is a power, not a quality, and powers help her more than qualities do. Nature heals, and the physician is her servant. The body of a simple medicine is thrown away and only its hidden power, its invisible body, is kept. [CH4.6]
6. The worst diseases grow from seeds, invisible bodies and invisible spirits, not from the body's matter. As all medicine comes from three parts, there are three kinds of disease, each cured by one of the three. [CH4.6; CH4.10]
7. The physician must be skilled in separating the pure from the impure. [CH5.12]
8. Our reading is that the description of man is joined to the True Physic because the medicine works on man as on a little world made of all things. [OPEN.24; CH4.6; MICRO.12]
9. The author says he has not dealt with everything, and that by the law of philosophers some things are left obscure. [CH6.76]

## What the author offers the reader

The author offers the reader two of the finest secrets of the art of separating. He says he first tested every preparation of them with his own hands, after mistakes that cost him money, time and work. [OPEN.3]

He refuses to fill a huge volume with copied-out formulas for mixed medicines. A margin note says that the biggest formulas are, for the most part, the biggest deceits. He gathered many descriptions from the men he sought out, and he reduced them all to a single one, which he first tested by his own chemical trial. [OPEN.3; N005]

He says he examined what he gathered with the help of Vulcan. This can be read two ways. In one, Vulcan stands for the work of fire: the book speaks of chemistry as Vulcan's art, and it names a school of Vulcan in the same passage as medicines that must be prepared and tried by fire. In the other, a margin note makes Vulcan one and the same spirit with the invisible body of things and with nature's own art. [OPEN.3; CH3.8; CH6.76; N123]

Our reading is that two things here already point to the True Physic: preparations proved by his own trial, and many descriptions reduced to one tested one. The reason is that a margin note in the third chapter makes a like contrast, setting medicine duly prepared by separation against the mixtures sold in shops. [OPEN.3; N172]

The author expects that the philosophers who kept these secrets in silence will accuse him of breaking open the seal of chemistry. [OPEN.10]

His answer is that the true heirs of wisdom will find far better treasures by asking, seeking and knocking, with tireless work and watchfulness. Those who are lazy and despise these secrets will never lay hands on them, because they are unfit for work with their hands. [OPEN.10]

He also says that the mysteries are mixed together in a fitting place. What this means stays open. [OPEN.10]

## What the True Physic is

The first heading asks what the medicine is that cures people's diseases, which few physicians know. The first chapter answers it under the title of the True Physic. The book spells the word physic in two ways. [OPEN.24; CH1.0]

That chapter teaches that no natural body can be split, by any skill of dissolving, into more or fewer than three parts. The book names them mercury, sulphur and salt, and calls them a liquid, an oil and salt. The author grounds this in creation: the threefold God spoke a threefold word of making and so made all things threefold. The art of separating shows this plainly. [CH1.19]

A margin note explains this separating as parting purity from impurity, or truth from falsehood. Like words come back later in the duty of the physician, who must separate the pure from the impure. [N052; CH5.12]

The author says those skilled in the art can show this by plain experience and with certainty. His example is green wood, which has three kinds of moisture. Fire consumes the watery one and the oily one. The third, the salt, is greasy, very small in amount, thin and lasting, and it remains in the ashes. [CH1.19]

Each of the three has its task. The salt sets bodies firm and gives them solidity, colour and taste. The sulphur gives the body substance and change. The mercury gives things their powers. [CH1.19]

The same three make the body of man. The earth, endowed with salt, mercury and sulphur, is the cause of man's material body. [CH1.19]

A margin note says that when the salt, or mummy, is spent, things breed nothing but worms; one word in the note cannot be read. The word mummy can be read two ways. In one, it is a second name for the salt, the lasting part, since the note sets the two names side by side as one. In the other, it is the preserving power in a body: the fourth chapter says nature heals herself by her inborn mummy and calls it her inward balm. [N053; CH4.6]

Our reading is that the names mercury, sulphur and salt do not mean only the common substances. The reason is that the book explains them as a liquid, an oil and a lasting part left in the ashes, and finds them in green wood, where the oily part is only like brimstone. So the names stand for three parts that every body has. [CH1.19]

## Man as a little world, added to the first heading

The author adds to the first heading a description of man as a little world, which he calls almost forgotten and unknown. [OPEN.24]

Man is made of all bodies and created things. All the properties of animals, plants and minerals entered into him. In the same way, all outward things are nothing but man's body. [MICRO.12; MICRO.24]

Man has two bodies, as Adam had: a visible one made of the elements, and an invisible one that comes from the heavens. [MICRO.31]

These invisible bodies are not the seven visible lights in the sky; those lights are only their bodies. In growing things, the seed of a grain of wheat is such an invisible body. Visible bodies are nothing but what these invisible bodies cast off. [MICRO.32]

A margin note gives these invisible bodies a part in disease: they send the plague into us through our skin, as the sun sends its beams through glass. [N124]

Our reading is that this description bears on the True Physic. The reason is a chain the book lays out in three places: the medicine is a particular power hidden in seeds; a seed is an invisible body; and in a cure only the medicine's invisible body is taken. The fourth chapter also says man is called a little world because the whole world keeps, feeds and cures him. That this is why the description is added is our reading. [CH2.2; MICRO.32; CH4.6; OPEN.24]

## Where the True Physic lies hidden

The second heading asks where the True Physic lies hidden and can be found, and the second chapter answers it. [OPEN.25; CH2.0]

It is found in all three kinds of natural things: plants, animals and minerals. The author adds that these commonly give medicines enough to keep health and cure all diseases of the body. In them lies the true and particular medicine for bodily disorders. [CH2.2]

It is not a quality. It does not lie in the four outward qualities, and the author notes that Theophrastus speaks learnedly of this. It is a particular power hidden in the very seeds, arising by nature without art. The Creator planted it in every growing thing at the beginning, by the word through which all things were brought out of darkness into light. [CH2.2]

Our reading is that nature makes this power and the art only frees it from its cover. The reason is that the book says the power arises without art, yet the next heading says it must be got out by fire. [CH2.2; CH3.0]

In the last chapter the author wishes that each worker be set to search one of these three kinds of natural things. [CH6.30]

Our reading is that man, made of the properties of animals, plants and minerals, is cured from the same three kinds of things. The reason is that the book makes man of these three and finds the medicine in them. [MICRO.12; CH2.2; CH6.30]

## How it is got out and prepared by fire

The third heading says the True Physic is covered with bark and rind and must be got out and rightly prepared by fire. [CH3.0]

Our reading is that bark and rind stand for the outward body of a thing, and the kernel for its hidden power. The reason is that the book says people in Galen's day did not know how to separate the bark and husk from the kernel, and the fourth chapter says the body of simple medicines is to be thrown away. This is the narrowest reading. [CH3.40; CH4.6]

Chemical skill is needed for any medicine: without it, all medicine is lifeless. The author blames physicians who leave the making of their medicines to a careless and greedy apothecary, to the great harm of their patients. [CH3.8]

By breaking bodies down in this way, the properties that lay hidden in them are brought to light. So a physician should practise much at this true analysis and living anatomy of bodies, because every lasting quality of a body is found in its salt, mercury or sulphur. [CH3.8]

A margin note gives reasons why medicine duly prepared by this art should be preferred to the mixed medicines in the shops. The first is that many sick people will take it more easily and willingly. [N172; CH3.17]

The book names the way: making fine, drawing out and separating. It says this chemical way was still imperfect and little used in Galen's day. Galen, as the author reports him, tried to find a way to separate the contrary parts of vinegar, as of milk, and could have done so had he been skilled in the art of distilling. [CH3.40]

A margin note gives the three first things of milk as whey, butter and cheese, and says all that is earthy is salt. The author says Galen would gladly have served Theophrastus (Paracelsus) to learn how to separate the three first parts of vinegar, and above all to learn how to prepare the highest preparations, which the book names magisteries and elixirs. [N181; CH3.40]

The book calls these three first parts of vinegar qualities, in brackets. This allows two readings. In one, the bracketed word is an added gloss, and the three first are the same three parts as in the first chapter, as the milk note's remark that all that is earthy is salt suggests. In the other, the word means qualities, as it says, though the second chapter denies that the True Physic lies in qualities. Both readings rest on a single English word of the translation. [CH3.40; N181; CH2.2]

People neglect the art for gold: almost everyone burns to make gold but does not care to prepare medicines properly. A margin note names four pillars of medicine: philosophy, alchemy, astronomy, and the medicine's own power. [CH3.43; N185]

The author calls this art divine, most wonderful and proven. Rough people have much disparaged it. Yet abuse does not condemn it: the better a thing is, the more harmful its abuse. [CH3.45]

## How medicine works on the body

The fourth heading asks how, and with what power, medicine works on the body and cures its diseases. [CH4.0]

Like cures like. Medicines should not be contrary to the sick part but agree with it. The medicine has outwardly the same nature that the sick part has inwardly, so that the inner nature which needs help is strengthened by the abundance of the outer one. When what earth, air, water and fire bring forth in man falls sick, it must be restored by what they bring forth in the great world. [CH4.6]

Nature heals, and the physician serves her. Nature heals her own weaknesses by an inborn power within her. When her inner strength fails, her servant the physician must give her outward help. Sometimes it is best to leave the work to nature's own art alone. A margin note adds that the inward physician does the work when the natural physician fails; this rests on the note alone and on its exact words. [CH4.6; N188]

Power, not quality. Nature is a power, not a quality, and without power a quality is dead and merely accidental. Opium, which is very cold, saves some people in the plague not by its coldness but by its particular poisonous power, which is stronger than the hidden poison of the plague. So nature destroys one poison by another and overcomes a weak evil with a stronger one. [CH4.6]

Our reading is that the author's allowance that contraries also cure does not clash with like curing like. The reason is that the book itself draws the line between power and quality: what must not be contrary is the medicine's nature to the part's nature, while one power may be set against the power of the disease, as with opium. The book says contraries cure because they make up what is lacking. [CH4.6]

Where diseases come from. The roots of diseases are neither hot nor cold. The most harmful diseases do not spring from the matter of the body or from what the book calls the four humours, but from seeds, from invisible bodies, and from invisible spirits of the three parts that work like craftsmen. The ancients did not know these invisible makers of disease. [CH4.6]

So the medicine must be a spirit too. Medicine is a spirit, not a body, and only the wise can discern it. The body of simple medicines is thrown away, because life must work on life and spirit on spirit, once the impure body is separated off. The book calls this the true and living anatomy. [CH4.6]

Our reading is that here the fourth chapter ties back to the third. The reason is that throwing away the body of a medicine and keeping its hidden power is what the bark-and-rind heading promises, and the true and living anatomy of the fourth chapter matches the true analysis and living anatomy of bodies in the third. [CH4.6; CH3.8; CH3.0]

The cure goes to the root. When the centre, root and seed of a disease are pulled out, the work is done. A physician who cures by what the book calls complexion is like a man who puts out the harmless flame and leaves the fire burning in the coals. [CH4.6]

Whose words these sayings about the root are can be read two ways. Margin notes beside them name Paracelsus and his writings. In one reading the words are the author's own, and the notes point to where Paracelsus says the same. In the other they are Paracelsus's words, as the notes beside them suggest. [CH4.6; N189; N191]

The three parts of the first chapter come back as a rule of cure. As all medicine comes from mercury, sulphur and salt, there are three kinds of disease, one for each. All ulcers are cured by mercury, all sticky running sores by salt, and all inflammations by sulphur. The author says this needs no reasons to prove it. [CH4.10]

## What kind of physician this medicine needs

The fifth heading asks what kind of physician this medicine requires to serve it. [OPEN.28]

The physician must be skilled in the art of separating: one who can part the pure from the impure and bring the patient back to health by a wise alchemical preparation. A margin note says the philosopher and the physician spring from one another. [CH5.12; N202]

The art is older than Paracelsus, who was not its author. He sought to restore the true and philosophical medicine, which no one else in his time tried to restore, and which had been lost and wiped out for many years. [CH5.14]

Others followed him. The author hopes their writings will help readers, by daily practice of piety, to draw out the great treasure that is hidden and shut up within themselves. [CH5.20]

How this relates to the True Physic can be read two ways. In one, the inner treasure is a further good set beside the medicine: these men studied the medicine, and their writings lead to the inner life. In the other, drawing out the inner treasure is the same course as drawing out the medicine, getting out what is hidden, but read in man; the book's words for drawing out and for hidden echo the headings on getting the medicine out and on where it lies hidden. [CH5.20; OPEN.26; OPEN.25]

Who may give medicine. Many physicians cannot bear that medicine and its preparations be shared with all sorts of people. The author calls the belief behind their fear, that a secret loses its power once it is known, a foolish notion of the common crowd. Yet neither God nor nature has made every man a physician. A margin note adds that Paracelsus would not have medicine kept hidden. [CH5.62; N227]

The ground beneath it all is faith. A margin note says the best foundation of medicine is faith in God and love of one's neighbour, and without these all art is worth nothing. [N227]

## The work the author wishes for, and his closing words

The author wishes that the great ones of his time would spend part of their wealth on men who are pious, conscientious and skilled in chemistry. Each should be set to search one of the three kinds of natural things, to draw out the choicest medical secrets and separate them by fire into their three first parts. [CH6.30]

Our reading is that this wish repeats the whole course of the True Physic in its own words. The reason is that it names the three kinds of things of the second chapter, the drawing out and the fire of the third, and the three parts of the first and fourth. It stands in the last chapter and speaks of secrets in the plural, not of the one chief medicine which that chapter is about. [CH6.30; CH2.2; CH3.0; CH1.19; CH4.10]

The work is costly. The Prince paid the author's necessary expenses, since the author could not bear alone the burden of all these medicines, which must be prepared and tried by fire. [CH6.76]

Medicine has not yet reached perfection, and much remains for later ages to discover. Of the secrets by which life was once lengthened, the author says that we lack nothing but the knowledge of them and their use. [CH6.76; CH6.71]

He holds some things back. He says he has shared openly only as far as was lawful and allowed by God. He has not dealt with everything, to avoid being long-winded. And by the law of philosophers, some hard things are left obscure to young students, for the wise to find out. [CH6.76]

His closing hope is that the ancient, true and philosophical medicine, not yet fully known because of the long wear of time, may be restored. This can be read two ways. In one, it is the True Physic under another name: the book calls what Paracelsus restored the true and philosophical medicine, also lost through time. In the other, it is the chief medicine, which the author gives a heading of its own in the last chapter. [CH6.76; CH5.14]
