# CHAP. III. How the True Physic, which is covered bark and rind, is to be got out, and rightly prepared by Fire

The heading names the subject: a “True Physick” that is “covered bark and rind”, how it “is to be got out”, and how it is “rightly prepared by Fire” [CH3.0]. The covering, the getting out and the Fire are the threads the chapter follows.

## Poison and Physick in the same thing

The chapter opens: “ALL things in respect of the first matter were created perfect, but the Chymist perfecteth the last matter by Fire” [CH3.1]. The margin gives the same work to the physician: “The Physitian perfecteth the Creature of God by Fire.” [N160].

The reason is that “nothing in this lower world (which is subject to generation and corruption) is so noble but hath Poyson in it selfe” [CH3.1]. In God’s chief works “where there is hurt there is also help, where there is venome there is vertue” [CH3.1].

Witnesses follow. Firmianus is given the words that when God gave power to man, “presently he raised up an enemy, least the Power growing lazy should loose its Nature” [CH3.2]. Roger Bacon, “the English Phylosopher”, is given the image: where God “of his MERCY he appoynted plenty of Physick, even there also by the power of his incomprehensible Justice he substituted Poyson to guard it” [CH3.3]. Hermes, “in his Key”, with Ecclesiasticus, holds that “all sublunary things ought to consist of contraposition and contrariety” [CH3.4].

Reading of the guarded Physick. Stated: the margin says what the two are: “The severe Justice of God is the disease & venome in all things: on the contrary, the Mercy of God is the Physick in Nature and in all things.” [N163]. Interpreted (mine): the Poyson that guards the Physick is the covering the heading calls bark and rind; the narrowest support is that the chapter later calls the husks of Physick “enemyes to mans body” [CH3.14].

A margin note to the next paragraph names “The cause of Simpathy and Antipathy.” [N165]: God ordained “that Sympathy and Antipathy should be alike good”, to stir men “to contemplate and search out his secrets” [CH3.5]. Heraclitus “called Nature the daughter of War”, and Homer “called it Contention” [CH3.5].

## Man his own enemy; the physician a minister

The strife is in man too: “Man is his own enemy” [CH3.6]. In “the little world, the body, there lurketh perpetuall strife, in it lyeth hid the preserver and destroyer of health” [CH3.6].

Hence the physician’s office: “Nature hath commanded the Physitians to be ministers, and to seperate, not masters to compose” [CH3.6]. Remedies “require preparations, separations and exaltations before they can impart their hidden and restrained vertues” [CH3.6]. The virtues are there already; the work frees them.

## The trial by Fire: Chymistry

“As all things are proved by Fire, so also the Tryall of the knowledge of Physick is to be made by Fire: Physick and Chymistry cannot be separated” [CH3.7]. This Chymistry is “not that which Mountebanks use to paint Faces with” [CH3.7].

It works “in imatation of the Archaeon Ventricle or Naturall In-bred Chymist”, frees medicines “from those scurvy raggs wherein they were wrapt up”, so “that pure and Christiline matter may be administred to our bodies” [CH3.7]. Freeing it is deliverance “from prison and captivity”, a hard task [CH3.7].

Reading of the rags and the prison. Stated: the same sentence gives the plain sense, “a due separation from the impurities and corruptible and filthy mixture of superficiall and externall Elements” [CH3.7]; the rags are that outward mixture. Interpreted (mine): the prisoner is the pure matter, which the next sentence calls “this” [CH3.7].

Without this, “all Physick is but livelesse” [CH3.8]. The author rejects common physicians “who patronize their sloth under other mens paines and study” and leave preparation to “some carelesse and covetous Apothecary”, but excepts “the conscientious Apothecaries” [CH3.8]. A physician should be practised in “this true Analysis and vitall Anatomy of bodies”, because “there is no constant quality of any body which is not to be found either in the Salt, or Mercury, or Sulphur of the same body” [CH3.8].

## Three orders and three First things

First, “all compound bodies of the inferiour Globe are to be distributed into three orders or companies; into Animalls, Vegetables, and Mineralls” [CH3.9], each examined to its parts, to find “the three First things (viz. Salt, Sulphur, and Mercury)” in each [CH3.9].

## How impurity came into things

“The first face of Things was pure, sound, perfect without corruption and death” [CH3.10]. After “the transgression and fall from unity to alterity”, “new Tinctures came in (even infinite evills)”, and “the beauty of the whole Creature was transplanted” [CH3.11]. The margin explains: “The transplantation of the Creature is by the calamity and coming in of sicknesse upon it” [N169]. So “Impurity was joyned to the pure roots, which was the predestination of diseases” [CH3.11].

Hence “Nature therefore, as it is now, gives us nothing that is pure in the world” [CH3.12], as a spur, “that we should begin to learn the knowledge of Chymistry from our cradles” [CH3.12]. Shut out of Paradise, “we ought to till and manure the EARTH, to wit, the whole frame of the world” [CH3.12], in “this LAND of LABOUR” [CH3.12].

Reading of the tilling. Stated: EARTH here means “the whole frame of the world”, tilled “by admiration, searching into, and knowledge” [CH3.12]. The margin to the next paragraph says: “He that hath learned to know God & himselfe hath ordered the Earth with good husbandry” [N170].

This is “the true end of Mans Creation”: to manage the Earth, “recovering what he hath lost” [CH3.13].

## The Separatory art: kernel from husk

“But to passe from Food let us come to Physick” [CH3.14]. No one will dislike “this Separatory art which teacheth rightly to discerne and seperate the Good from the Bad” [CH3.14], and a row of pairs follows: “Poyson from healing Medicines and Balsoms, Light from Darknesse, Life from Death” [CH3.14].

Reading of the heading’s bark and rind. Stated: the art separates “that which is pure Celestiall, the kernall and Marrow” from “the Rinds, and Membranes, the Covers, Shells, Husks and dreggs” [CH3.14], which are “the Coate aed Cottage of Physick which are enemyes to mans body” [CH3.14]. What they cover is “the Soul the Inhabitant thereof, the Super-elementall mystery the Quint-Essence” [CH3.14]. Interpreted (mine): the True Physick of the heading is this Quint-Essence, its bark and rind these husks; both places speak of Physick under a covering.

Every venomous thing has a Balsom: “there is no poysonous Creature but hath in it an Antidote against its own poyson” [CH3.14]. But “these Formes of Physick work better when they are extracted, then whilst they are drowned in Matter” [CH3.14]. The margin: “As Death separateth eternall and perishing things, so doth Fire the good from the bad, the Quint-Essence from the body.” [N171]. It also says “Putrefications onely are the true Correctives of all Physick.” [N171].

The ground is the Form: “by how much the more it hath of the Form, by so much the more is there of the Entity” [CH3.15].

## Three advantages

“Hence many advantages follow.” [CH3.16]. First, “many sick people will take it more easily and willingly” [CH3.17] than “muddy and pudly potions” [CH3.17]. Second, “Though these medicines be often used, yet they hurt not the stomack” [CH3.18]. Third, “all the hurtfull quality is altogether cast out of, or at least easily kept under in these Essences” [CH3.19].

So “he can be no safe Physitian who hath it not”: one simple may hold contrary substances, “as we see in Opium and honey” [CH3.19]. Only this art lets the Galenists defend their axiom that “all bitter things are hot” [CH3.19].

Reading of the knife. Interpreted (mine; wording-dependent): in “The knot of this Answer must be cut with an Anatomicall knife fire” [CH3.19] the knife is Fire, and the anatomy is the separation CH3.8 calls “vitall Anatomy of bodies” [CH3.8]. The result is stated: “in Opium there is a sweet Narcotick Sulphur, and a bitter hot Salt” [CH3.19].

Even the poison of metals may be “corrected and amended by the help of this Art and of Fire” [CH3.20]. The margin: “Poyson reduced to its Arcanum is not poyson, but a soveraign nedicine” [N173].

## Minerals and stones above Vegetables

Minerals and precious stones, rightly prepared, “doe much excell all Vegetables in effecting cures” [CH3.21]. Reasons follow: Vegetables subject to corruption cannot free the body from it “as the Spirits of perfect mettalls doe” [CH3.22]; imperfect metals already heal, so “perfect things doubtlesse have received of the Omnipotent far greater force and vertues” [CH3.23]; plants and living things cannot “admit of the solid and stable Nature of a Balsom” [CH3.24]; and a long generation gives stones “a greater faculty of working”, while “perfect bodies are generated in a shorter space” [CH3.25].

By the way, stones take Tincture from metals “by formation of the Stars, for they are transplanted Mertalls” [CH3.27]. The margin: “Gemmes are Elementary Stars.” [N174].

## One seed, one Nature, one Spirit

The author answers those who say “mettalls are of no use in Physick, at least in the civill life” [CH3.28], a view he reports to reject: “the Animall, Vegetable and Minerall sperm or seed have but one rise or originall, and differ onely in Quality of Place and Receptacle” [CH3.28].

That one kind “is the generall seed of all things or subject of the first matter” [CH3.29]. “All things are from one Principle, and tendeth to one” [CH3.30], as in “Anaxagoras his Pamsperm, which Aristotle unjustly condemned” [CH3.30]. The Universal Spirit “liveth in and quickneth this whole Masse” [CH3.31]; Avicen calls it “the Soule of the world powred into all things” [CH3.31]. The author bounds this: “without any superstition or Idolatrous worship, giving honour onely to One God” [CH3.31].

In his own voice: “Nature, I say, is that medium which by an Harmonicall consent joyneth the lowest things to the highest” [CH3.32]. His witness is a child born “with a gilded tooth in the left and lower jaw, which I did both see and handle” [CH3.32]. Those of “more hid and secret Phylosophy” know “that same Spirit and minerall Nature which produceth Gold in the bowells of the Earth is also in Man” [CH3.33].

## Witnesses: the matter in man

Albertus Magnus is given that gold was found “in the bodyes and heads of some that were hanged” [CH3.34]; “the Phylosophers say that the matter of their Mystery may be had every where” [CH3.34]. Morienes, asked by King Calid for the matter of the Elixer, answers “It is of thee O King, said he” and “thou are in its Mine” [CH3.36]. Raymund Lully “got his matter out of a mean and worthlesse thing” [CH3.36]. Riplaeus à Portis: it “is of little worth, and may be got out of its Mine by art” [CH3.37]. Rhasis: “in every thing there is every thing potentially” [CH3.38].

Reading of where the matter is. Several readings. (a) It is in man: Morienes’s “of thee” [CH3.36], Riplaeus [CH3.37], and the mineral virtue “in the head among the teeth” [CH3.35]. (b) It is in every thing, man one case: the Phylosophers [CH3.34], Rhasis [CH3.38], and the margin, where “The matter of a stone is said to be in every thing” [N180] (note-only).

“But these things are by the by.” [CH3.39].

## Galen and Paracelsus

In Galen’s days “they knew not how to separate the bark and husk from the kernell” [CH3.40]. Galen, in his reported words, tried to “discern the hot parts of vineger from the cold” [CH3.40], and might have succeeded “if he had been well skild in the Distillatory Art” [CH3.40]. This is no disgrace: God and Nature “doe communicate particular gifts to severall ages” [CH3.40].

Had Galen lived when Paracelsus did, “he would not have envyed, but reverenced his learning” [CH3.40]. Against Paracelsus stand physicians “who have not learned so much as their A. B. C. in the Spagyrick Physick” [CH3.40]; their words, which the author rejects, call him “a circumforaneous or rambling rayler” [CH3.40]. The author calls him “the immortall glory of Germany” [CH3.40]. Interpreted (mine): Theophrastus [CH3.41] is the same man, since CH3.40 passes from Paracelsus to “Theophraestus” in one sentence [CH3.40].

Reading of Paracelsus’s Secrets. Stated: had he been borne with, “he would have more clearly have manifested to thankful posterity his Secrets which God disclosed to him” [CH3.41]. The true medicines “require solutions, mortifications, cohibitions, resuscitations, &c. truly Phylosophycall” [CH3.41].

## Why the true medicines are few

“But truly I think this wicked generation is not worthy of such medicines.” [CH3.42]. God “will never suffer those Secrets to be known, especially in these corrupt and perilous times” [CH3.42].

Almost all men “have a burning desire after making Gold” [CH3.43], not knowing “the Metaphysicks of Paracelsus, the true Phylosophy” [CH3.43]. Most Chymists are turned aside “by the glitter of Court-service” [CH3.44]. So “this Divine Spagyrie” is disparaged [CH3.45]; but “the bettter the thing is, the more destructive is the abuse of it” [CH3.45].

## Closing prayer

The chapter ends: “O fountaine of Truth and Wisedome, consider our condition” [CH3.46], hoping God will stir up some to “confute the delusions and errours of the schoolmen not with words, but deeds” [CH3.46], “the time of Revocation and Regeneration drawing nigh” [CH3.46].
