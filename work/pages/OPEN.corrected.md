# The Admonitory Preface of Oswald Crollie, Physician: to the Most Illustrious Prince Christian Anhaltin

The heading addresses the Preface to Prince Christian Anhaltin [OPEN.0], but the first words speak to the reader: “COURTEOUS READER” [OPEN.1].

The section moves in four steps: why the author breaks the old silence about secrets; what he offers and how he tested it; the enemies he expects, group by group; and a numbered list of what the Preface will handle.

## Breaking the rule of silence

He opens with the case against himself. The ancient Philosophers, by the example of Actaeon, “have strictly forbidden to prophane the Secrets, and rashly to prostitute the Treasures of Nature to unworthy and filthy minded men” [OPEN.2]. That rule is theirs (voice: QUOTED, the ancient Philosophers); he reports it under “ALTHOUGH” and sets himself against it with “Notwithstanding” [OPEN.2].

His ground is God’s example: the Heavenly Father shines on all, “for he communicateth his light, without respect or envy, to the good and bad” [OPEN.2], and “We are bound to immitate him whose Children we ought to be” [OPEN.2]. A margin saying, under the name Victor, adds: “The gifts of God grow, & are improved by communicating” [N003].

Stated: he has kept something hidden until now. He will no longer “hide as in a Napkin, the Talent committed to me by the Father of Lights, by an impious silence” [OPEN.2]. Even so, the opening is to some: “the Gates of Wisdome ought alwayes to stand open to ingenious men” [OPEN.2].

## What he offers, and how he came by it

He will “set before thee two of the most excellent Spagyrik Mysteries” [OPEN.3], brought “out of the most deep secret of my heart” [OPEN.3]. Their preparations “have all of them been first proved by my own handy experience” [OPEN.3]. He refuses to “fill up a huge Volume with the copies of large Receipts” [OPEN.3]; the margin adds, “The greatest Receipts the greatest Deceits for the most part” [N005].

Much of his material was gathered “for the space of almost twenty years” of travel, from men learned in Chymistry, “partly by intreaty, partly by purchase and permutation” [OPEN.3]; other things he found himself “in the practice of Physick” [OPEN.3]. A margin note says: “Some of these have beene prepared by others for the Emperor Rudolph to whom I voluntarily imparted them” [N004] (note-only).

He tested before he published: he would “first take my eyes in my hand, as I may so say” [OPEN.3], marking that phrase as a figure, and “by the help of Vulcan search and diligently examine the chief and choycest things” [OPEN.3]. Interpreted: Vulcan stands for fire, since the next paragraphs speak of “Nature's Vulcan” and of “Pyrotechnye, or the Art of Fire and Chymistry” [OPEN.4; OPEN.5]. His aim is “that so the Children of knowledge, those searchers out and lovers of Truth may attain that at a cheap rate which hath cost me dear the learning” [OPEN.3]. Many boasted secrets disappointed him: in exchanges the others “got an Egg for a Nut”, and he found “nothing but a worm-eaten kernel” [OPEN.3].

## Trial by fire against hearsay

Of those who wrote in his time of the secret preparations “from other mens relation & not their own handy experience” [OPEN.4], he says the greater part brought students only lost labour. Since “many things fall out between the Theory and the Practice” [OPEN.5], “no man is further to be believed, then as every one findeth by his own proper experience” [OPEN.5]: by the trial of fire, “which discerneth and separateth that which is false, from that which is true” [OPEN.5].

## Few medicines, and one set apart

A saying given to Damascen (voice: QUOTED, Damascen) grounds the choice of few things: “Let thy Medicines be few, whose vertue and operations thou hast often tryed” [OPEN.6]. Nothing in Nature, he claims, is more choice than what he offers, “except only that universall and most famous Medicine which the most ancient wise Philosophers had from the beginning of the world” [OPEN.6]. Interpreted: that Medicine belongs to some only, since the Philosophers “have extoll'd it as a miracle to their just and lawfull Heirs” [OPEN.6]. Going further needs work and grace: “if the grace of the most high favour be infused into him” [OPEN.6].

## The readers he means

He admits: “I have proved but some of those virtues and operations” assigned to each medicine [OPEN.7]. This sits beside his claim that all the preparations were proved by his own hands [OPEN.3]; one place speaks of preparations, the other of virtues and operations. For the rest he trusts readers able to judge, “the Spagyriks, or those who know how to discern between true and false” [OPEN.7]: so the book explains its own word Spagyrik, and later speaks of “that Spagyricall or Divinely-distinguishing Sanctuary” [OPEN.11]. These are “the Sons of Art, who know the tearms and bounds thereof” [OPEN.7], men “who can even by a glance and sight only make a good construction and easily guesse of these things” [OPEN.7]. Stated: the work is pitched to experts, for “these preparations doe require such an Artist” [OPEN.7].

They keep apart from the common rabble of Alchymists because of a slander, “The errors of the Artists are imputed to the Art” [OPEN.7], whose speaker the text does not name (voice: UNCLEAR).

The true physicians are to use the medicines “rightly prepared by their own labour” and not trust “the Sophistical and fraudulent preparations of others” [OPEN.8]. They will find “far greater efficacies and operations by the cures they doe, then I can or ought to set down and assign” [OPEN.8]. Interpreted (wording-dependent, on the one word ought): he limits what he writes, not only what he knows.

## The critics foreseen

He meets the danger of publishing with “the buckler of my sincerity” [OPEN.9], and numbers the groups he expects against him.

### 1. The Mystical Hermetic Philosophers

“The Mysticall Hermetick Philosophers who heretofore in part have pryed into these excellent secrets, will rise against me” [OPEN.10]. Their complaint, reported to be answered (voice: QUOTED, these Philosophers), is that things “hitherto concealed in deep silence; should so soon be brought to light, and made common to every one” [OPEN.10]. “They will accuse me for breaking open the seale of Chymistry” [OPEN.10], and for forgetting the Rule of Hippocrates, “To impart sacred things to sacred persons” [OPEN.10].

His answer: as heirs of wisdom they should be “without envy, or at least ought so to be” [OPEN.10], and they “shall know that in the true Cabala, Magick and Woarchadumie, there are laid up far better Treasures” [OPEN.10]. A margin note here reads “A word I doe not understand” [N015]; it names no word, and its voice is UNCLEAR (note-only). The way to those Treasures is the Oratory and Laboratory: “I mean by asking, seeking, and knocking with unwearied paines and watchfullnesse” [OPEN.10].

These Treasures are hidden only for a time: they are “to be manifest indifferently to all in due time by the light of Grace and Nature” [OPEN.10], namely “after the Bloody Judgement of the Son is first finished, which undoubtedly will follow the Watry (Judgement) of the Father, then in the third Age of the Holy Spirit” [OPEN.10]. Then comes “Elias the Artist” [OPEN.10], one “who is to restore all things” [OPEN.10]. He gathers the Ages from “the twelve Articles of the Apostles Creed, divided into three parts, very fitly answering to so many houres of the great and One Day, viz. The continuance of this world” [OPEN.10]; Stated, the text says what the One Day is.

What he publishes now he calls small: “these present crums to our thankfull posterity” [OPEN.10]. Yet he also says nothing in Nature is more choice than his offering, the universal Medicine excepted [OPEN.6].

The secrets also guard themselves. The slothful fail “by reason of their indisposition and unfitnesse to manuall operation” [OPEN.10], and the profane will hardly grasp “these Divine revealed Mysteries, which are mingled together in a fit place” [OPEN.10], things “which the wise-hearted can apprehend in few words” [OPEN.10]. Several defensible readings of “mingled together in a fit place” [OPEN.10]: (a) the Mysteries are set among other matter so that the unworthy pass them by, since the sentence is about the profane failing to discern them; (b) they are simply gathered in their proper place in the book, taking “fit place” [OPEN.10] in its plain sense. The margin adds: “Only they that are worthy shall understand these things” [N018].

So the Preface holds two things together: the duty to give like a Father who shines on good and bad [OPEN.2], and the claim that only the worthy will understand [OPEN.10; N018].

### 2. The Theophrasteans

Second come “the spurious and adulterous Theophrasteans (the worst and wickedest sort of men)” [OPEN.11]. Publication spoils their trade of “their cheating impostures up and downe the Country” [OPEN.11]. Their own are “forged Cachochymicall and Sophisticall Arts, which have not so much as a spark of truth in them” [OPEN.11]. Through such Cheaters “the most laudable and honourable name of true and noble Alchymistry, hath been unworthily made infamous and odious” [OPEN.11]. The margin: “These are not the vices of the Art, but of men abusing the Art” [N020].

### 3. The Galenists

Third, “the more single and subtle part of the Galenists, who like Nicodemus are of a milder temper” [OPEN.12], who “for fear of being excommunicated by some Athenian Rabbies, durst not openly professe the truth” [OPEN.12]. He expects them to welcome the light, but fears envy: “they will a little coldly commend those things, which notwithstanding are secretly reserved for their uses” [OPEN.12].

Then “the more dull and drossie sort of them” [OPEN.13], Interpreted as still Galenists, since the paragraph follows on the milder part. They are “plunged into Scholasticall dirt and dust over head and ears” [OPEN.13] and take their weapons from “their Captain, that railing Binarius” [OPEN.13]. The only reason he gives why their rage “should be sent to the Isle Anticyra” is “their ignorance of the just and due preparation of things” [OPEN.13]. The margin: “There is a two-fold Alchymy. Naturall, much esteemed by Artist. Sophisticall, as much detested by them.” [N023].

#### The Doctors and their titles

The Scholastic Doctors “who for want of wit could never find the way into Natures Sanctuary” [OPEN.14] condemn skilled physicians who lack degrees, titles “with which neither Hippocrates nor Galen, nor any Phisitians that we read of in their time were indowed or adorned” [OPEN.14]. The good physicians refuse them “least they should be made to swear to the Heathenish errors of the School-Gods” [OPEN.14]. Once “the spur of virtue, and the deserved reward of Learning”, titles are now, especially in Phisick, “bestowed either through flattery or bribery” [OPEN.15] on many unworthy of them. Their holders in time may come to question Galen’s method, seeing “the end doth not answer his beginning in the great Cures of Diseases” [OPEN.15]. The remedy: “they ought to become Schollars and servants, not Doctors and Masters of Nature” [OPEN.16], for “God onely is Lord and Master of Nature” [N027].

#### The converts in old age

Old physicians came to him complaining of studies in which, “like those that feed on Crabs and Crevises, they find much shell but little meat” [OPEN.17]. Some chose “to put off their old skin, like the wise Serpent, and putting on a new one, to become Schollars of Chymistry” [OPEN.17]; Stated, the text applies the figure itself. They study “those Secrets which are both in God and Nature” [OPEN.17].

Two books are named: “the great book of Grace, wherein the eternall health of our soules consisteth” [OPEN.19], and that of Nature, with its treasures “wherein the most High hath laid up medicines for our greatest and worst diseases” [OPEN.19]. The margin names them otherwise: “God is the first book that teacheth us eternall life” [N030], and “The firmament or heaven with all therein is the second Naturall book conducing to a mortall life” [N031].

These converts leave “the dead shadows of meer outsides” [OPEN.20], glossed as “or the externall Galenicall qualities” [OPEN.20], and by “Phylosophicall sequestration of the Creatures, or Naturall things” [OPEN.20] gain skill at the sickbed. The text names that work but gives no step of it here. The margin states the end of the art: “the end of Phisick is not to scrape up money, but to discover the secrets of Nature, and the love of the Phisitian to the sick” [N034] (note-only).

#### The thieves, and the closed bath

Some of the rabble “every where forbid, as poisonous, those Phisicall Secrets” [OPEN.21] and call the Chymists “Collier-like-Phisitians” [OPEN.21] (voice: QUOTED, these opponents, reported to be rejected), yet have “a secret and subtle desire to learne those things” [OPEN.21]. For them the door is shut: “admission to the Bath of Diana is not to be granted, because Pythagoras forbids to put our victualls into a close stool” [OPEN.21], “nor should we bestow our choicest herbs on such as rudely rush into the Chymists garden” [OPEN.21]. Interpreted: the Bath and the herbs stand for what he offers, withheld from the unworthy, since the paragraph is about who may receive these secrets. Still he will not “shut these doors against those that seek after the true and Ancient Phisick” [OPEN.21], who “give place to Paracelsus according to his desert in practice and preparations” [OPEN.21].

He limits the attack: “neither will this Discourse offend any holy and Learned Phisitian” [OPEN.22]; it is aimed at those “who oppose the Chymicall verity against conscience” [OPEN.22].

## What the Preface will handle

Last, “before I come to describe the Remedies” [OPEN.23], he lists what is “to be explained by a Philosophicall Phisitian” [OPEN.23]:

- I. What Phisick cures and “few Phisitians know it”, with “a perfect Philosophicall Description of the little World Man, almost forgotten and unknown” [OPEN.24]. Several defensible readings: what is almost forgotten may be the Man, since the words follow “the little World Man” [OPEN.24] directly, or the Description, since that is what the list item offers.
- II. “Where that True Phisick lyeth hid, and may be found.” [OPEN.25]
- III. “That it is to be fetcht out, and prepared truly, by fire.” [OPEN.26]
- IV. How “that Phisick worketh on mans body, and expelleth his diseases” [OPEN.27].
- V. “What manner of Phisitian that Phisick doth require for its Minister.” [OPEN.28]
- VI. The universal Medicine, “commended of many, possessed, yea seen, or believed of very few” [OPEN.29].
- Then “Some thing in defence of the published Truth, as an Epilogue or Conclusion.” [OPEN.30]

The list keeps the theme of hiding: true Phisick “lyeth hid” [OPEN.25], and the chief Medicine, set apart earlier [OPEN.6], is possessed by very few [OPEN.29].
